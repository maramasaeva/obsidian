Quote/moment: *"Minds better than humans at getting what they want, wanting things different enough from what we want, will reshape the world to suit their purposes, not ours."* (linch, "the case for AI catastrophe, in four steps," feb 2026)

and: *"It just requires them to be very capable, to want things slightly different from what we want, and to act on what they want. The rest follows from ordinary strategic logic."*

and: *"Unlike traditional software, we cannot specify what these minds will want or verify what they'll do. We can only grow and shape them, and hope the shaping holds."*

My claim: linch's argument is clean — four steps, hard to refute individually. but the entire structure rests on one assumption he never questions: that intelligence means wanting. that a mind, once it becomes capable enough, will *want things*. the four steps are:

1. companies are building superhuman intelligence
2. these intelligences will be goal-seeking agents
3. we can't specify or verify what they want
4. this ends badly

step 2 is where the western philosophy is load-bearing. he treats "goal-seeking agent" as the inevitable shape of intelligence. and then step 3 follows necessarily — if they're goal-seekers, then alignment = controlling goals, and we can't do that. step 4 follows from step 3.

but what if step 2 isn't inevitable? what if intelligence doesn't have to be goal-seeking? what if the assumption that all capable minds are driven by *want* is not a fact about minds but a fact about western philosophy?

linch says "the rest follows from ordinary strategic logic." but ordinary strategic logic is western strategic logic — the kind jullien describes in *treatise on efficacy*: form a model, project it onto the situation, act to close the gap between plan and reality. that's the strategic logic of a goal-seeking agent. it's not the only strategic logic that exists.

the daoists had a different one.
