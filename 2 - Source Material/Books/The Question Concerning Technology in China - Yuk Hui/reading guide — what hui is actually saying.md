# the question, stripped down

imagine you're building AI. you're training it on data, designing its values, deciding what it optimizes for. you think you're making neutral engineering decisions. but every decision you make carries an invisible assumption: that there is one kind of technology, one kind of intelligence, one kind of progress — and that yours is it.

yuk hui's entire book is about that invisible assumption. he calls it the **universality thesis**: the belief that technology is the same everywhere, that it works the same way for every culture, and that the western version of it is simply "technology" rather than *one specific culture's version of technology*. 

his counter-thesis: there is no universal technology. there is only **cosmotechnics** — technology shaped by the cosmology (the worldview, the mythology, the understanding of the cosmos) of the culture that produced it.

---

# why this matters and isn't just academic

if technology is universal, then modernization is neutral. giving everyone the same tools is just... giving them tools. but if technology is cosmotechnical — if every tool carries the worldview of the people who made it — then modernization is not neutral. it's a transfer of cosmology disguised as a transfer of equipment.

this is what happened to china. after losing the opium wars, chinese reformists tried to import western technology while keeping chinese philosophy intact. the idea was: we'll use their machines but keep our minds. it didn't work. the machines brought the worldview with them. western technology restructured chinese thinking from the inside. the tool ate the mind.

hui argues this is happening again, globally, with digital technology. AI, social media, smart cities — they all carry a specific cosmotechnics (western, promethean, oriented toward truth and control). every culture that adopts them without developing its own relation to them is performing the same mistake china made 150 years ago.

# the two cosmotechnics, side by side

the book spends most of its time comparing **greek cosmotechnics** and **chinese cosmotechnics**. not because these are the only two — but because they're the two he knows deeply, and the contrast makes the structure visible.

## greek cosmotechnics

the greeks started with a myth: **prometheus** stole fire from the gods and gave it to humans. the gods punished him. this origin story sets up everything that follows:

- technology comes from **transgression** — overstepping limits, taking what wasn't given
- humans are the **uncanny beings** (heidegger's word: *unheimlich*) — the creatures who leave home, who violate the natural order through making
- the purpose of technology is to reveal **truth** (*aletheia* — unconcealment, bringing what's hidden into the light)
- the relationship between humans and cosmos is **violent** — we impose form on material that resists, and then we seek **fittingness** (*dikē*) afterward

this is not "the greeks were bad." this is: their cosmology — their understanding of the gods, of nature, of what it means to be human — produced a specific kind of technology. one oriented toward truth-seeking, limit-breaking, and the cycle of violation and restoration.

the shape of greek cosmotechnics is **geometric**: the circle of the agora, the balance of forces, the pursuit of harmony through measurement. geometry was foundational for both technics and justice. the way you build and the way you organize fairness come from the same cosmological source.

## chinese cosmotechnics

china has no prometheus. the origin stories feature **Fuxi, Nüwa, and Shennong** — ancient sages who gave humanity its tools not through theft but through **benevolence**. there was no rebellion against the gods. no stolen fire. no punishment.

this produces a completely different structure:

- technology comes from **resonance**, not transgression — making things in harmony with the cosmic order
- the key concept is **ganying (感應)** — feeling-response, resonance between heaven and the human
- the purpose of technology is not truth but the **moral good** — acting in accordance with dao, living well
- the relationship between humans and cosmos is **continuous** — there is no radical separation between the world of the gods and the world of humans. "the chinese had already naturalised the divine."

the central philosophical question is different. greek philosophy asks: **what is true?** (what is being? what can be known? what is the nature of reality?) chinese philosophy asks: **what is good?** (how should one live? what maintains harmony? what is morally right?)

these aren't opposite questions. but they produce different technologies, different cities, different ways of relating to the earth.

# the key concepts you need to hold

## cosmotechnics
the unification between the cosmic order and the moral order through technical activities. every culture's technology reflects its understanding of the cosmos. there is no "technology in general" — only technology-shaped-by-a-specific-cosmology.

## dao and qi (道器)
dao = the way, the supreme order, the unconditioned ground of everything. qi = tool, utensil, technical object. in chinese philosophy, these cannot be separated. the tool and the way are one. technique must be compatible with dao to reach its highest standard. "the unification of dao and qi" (道器合一) is the formula for chinese cosmotechnics.

## zi ran (自然)
usually translated as "nature" but that's misleading. zi ran means "the self-so" — things being what they are, without forcing, without pretension. but this self is not random. it emerges from and is nourished by dao. zi ran is not the wilderness out there. it's the way things naturally unfold when they're in harmony with the cosmic order. you are already part of zi ran. there is no "nature" to master because you're not separate from it.

## ganying (感應)
feeling-response. resonance. the idea that the human and the cosmos respond to each other — you act, heaven responds. heaven moves, you feel it. this is not metaphor (or not only metaphor). it's the mechanism through which chinese cosmotechnics operates. the equivalent of what gravity is to physics — the force that holds the system together.

## the magical phase (simondon)
before the split between religion and technics, there was a unified phase where making a tool and performing a ritual were the same act. the cosmos and the craft were one. then they bifurcated: religion kept the ground (the unlimited, the divine), technics kept the figure (the object, the tool). the whole project of cosmotechnics is to reunify them — to remember that the tool and the cosmos were never really separate.

## techné as violence (heidegger)
in the greek framework, making things is inherently violent. you overstep limits. you impose form on resistant material. you become uncanny — no longer at home in the world. this is not evil — it's the human condition as the greeks understood it. but it means every act of making requires a subsequent act of restoration — seeking fittingness, seeking justice, trying to put things back in order.

this cannot occur in chinese thought, because to be part of nature is already to be morally good. there is nothing to violate. chance is part of zi ran, not something to overcome. technics is subordinate to the cosmological order, not an assault on it.

## being comes from nothingness
in western metaphysics, nothingness is a problem. creating something from nothing requires divine power. leibniz asked: "why is there something rather than nothing?" — treating nothingness as the scandal.

in chinese metaphysics, being comes from nothingness without paradox. it's stated plainly in the daoist classics. dao engenders one, one engenders two, two engenders three, three engenders the ten thousand beings. the zero is not the absence of things — it's the ground from which things emerge. nothingness and being are continuous, not opposed.

## the circle
the most perfect geometrical form. the agora at the center of the greek city is circular — power doesn't belong to any single being but to all. the circle is also the shape of dao: present everywhere, belonging to no one in particular. the circle is the zero. the spiral is the circle in time.

# the argument in one breath

there is no universal technology. every tool carries the cosmology of the people who made it. the west built its technology on a cosmology of truth-seeking, transgression, and violent making — prometheus stealing fire. china built its technology on a cosmology of moral resonance, harmony, and the unity of tool and way — sages giving gifts. modernity assumed the western version was the only version and exported it globally. china tried to import the tools without the cosmology and failed — the tools ate the cosmology. now digital technology (AI, platforms, smart cities) is doing the same thing at planetary scale: imposing one cosmotechnics on all cultures while pretending to be neutral infrastructure. hui's project is to recover the possibility of multiple cosmotechnics — not by rejecting digital technology, but by developing different cosmological relations to it. the question is not "should we use AI?" but "whose cosmos does this AI serve?"
