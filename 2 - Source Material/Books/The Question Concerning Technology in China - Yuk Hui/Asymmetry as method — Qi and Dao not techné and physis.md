Quote/moment: *"I do not use the usual translation of techné as Gong (工, 'work') or Ji (技, 'skill'), which would turn our inquiries into mere empirical examples; but rather start with a systematic view of Qi (器) and Dao (道), terms which, in turn, cannot be reduced to product (ergon) and soul (psyche). This asymmetry is presupposed and methodologically mobilised in this book."* (p. 54)

*"it would be incorrect to understand Yin-Yang as a dualism in the same sense that we use this term in Europe. The latter generally refers to two opposing and discontinuous entities: mind-body, culture-nature, being-nothingness. This form of dualism is not dominant in China, and Yin-Yang are not conceived of as two discontinuous entities. Hence in Chinese metaphysics there is virtually no problem in recognizing that being comes from nothingness, as is already stated in the Daoist classics."* (pp. 54–55)

and: *"Chinese thinking is fundamentally a relational thinking."* (p. 56) — concepts don't exist independently; they exist in relation, and transform over time within a broader system.

My claim: hui's method is itself a cosmotechnical move. he refuses to translate chinese concepts into western equivalents (techné = gong, physis = zi ran) because the translation *is* the colonialism. the moment you say "techné = gong," you've already imported the greek structure. the asymmetry — keeping qi-dao and techné-physis as genuinely different, not mirror translations — is the only way to let both cosmotechnics speak.

and this connects to [[Techné as violence — Heidegger on the Greek relation to making]] in a way that's easy to miss: the act of translation itself is a form of techné-as-violence. modernity's focus on truth drives it to find rational equivalences, to map everything onto a universal grid. translating dao as "the way" or qi as "tool" is an act of forcing — the same overstepping of limits that heidegger identifies as the essence of greek techné. the tool of translation colonizes because the cosmotechnics that built it is oriented toward truth and unconcealment, not toward living well and resonance. if your framework cared about the moral good instead of about what's "correct," you wouldn't need exact equivalences. you'd need something that transcends correctness.

the yin-yang point is crucial: western dualism is *discontinuous* (mind vs body, either/or). chinese "dualism" is *continuous* (yin-yang, both-and, emerging from each other). being comes from nothingness without paradox ([[Being comes from nothingness]]). this is not a deficiency — it's a different logical structure entirely. and it produces a different technics.
