Quote/moment: *"Poetry is also not technē, but in a different way, since a good poet is not the real author, but a channel for a divine power (theia moira)."* (p. 93, hui citing nussbaum on plato)

My claim: poetry escapes the technē/non-technē distinction entirely. it is not skill, but it is not chance either. the poet is not the author — the poet is the channel. divine power moves through them. they don't make the poem the way a carpenter makes a table. they receive it.

this is the greek version of what the chinese describe differently: the butcher pao ding doesn't cut the ox — the dao cuts through him ([[Pao Ding's knife — the butcher who follows Dao]]). skill at its highest level stops being skill and becomes receptivity. you stop imposing and start channeling. the technē dissolves into something else.

but there's a tension here. the greeks can only describe this dissolution as divine intervention — *theia moira*, divine power. it comes from outside, from above, from the gods. the poet is possessed. in chinese thought, the dissolution is not possession but harmony — you are not invaded by something external, you are aligned with something that was always there. the dao doesn't possess you. you stop resisting it.

two cosmotechnics of artistic creation: the western one needs a god to explain why the greatest art exceeds technique. the chinese one says technique was always subordinate to something larger, and the greatest practitioner simply recognizes that.
