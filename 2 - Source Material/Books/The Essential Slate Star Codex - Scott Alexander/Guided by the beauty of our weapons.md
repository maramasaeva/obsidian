Quote/moment: section IV of the essay asks the question that matters most for anyone trying to change minds:

*"The bigger question hanging over all of this: 'Do we have to find the solution — compelling narratives and documentaries — sounds easy in the Robinson solution... rhetoric, emotional appeals — also easy and fun for a certain type of person.'"* (p. 259)

section III has the surprise: a replication study on fact-checking found it actually *worked*. the researchers all went in with priors about backfire effects, adversarial framing, the idea that fact-checking would polarize. instead, all four found their priors were wrong. adversarial collaboration — both sides checking each other — actually moved people. but only when the teams were operating in good faith. *"All four of them found that fact-checking generally didn't backfire in most cases."* (p. 258)

scott then asks: if fact-checking can work in good faith, why doesn't it scale? because the conditions for adversarial collaboration are rare. you need two sides who genuinely want to test claims, not win arguments. prediction markets might be a mechanism. the media mostly isn't.

My claim: the Think Workshop faces this exact question. you can't just walk into a classroom and say "here's the evidence that screens destroy your attention." that's the fact-checking approach, and scott's showing it only works under narrow conditions (good faith, both sides open). the workshop needs the weapon — the narrative, the emotional entry point, the "stel u voor" moment that bypasses the defensive parsing. but the weapon has to *serve the truth*, not replace it. guided by the beauty of our weapons means choosing your rhetoric carefully enough that it carries the evidence inside it.

the question is whether teenagers are capable of the adversarial collaboration scott describes. jordi's reply ([[jordi on the already-fried problem]]) says no — their brains are already fried, they can't access the "before" state that would make the evidence land. so you need the weapon first. the evidence is the payload.
