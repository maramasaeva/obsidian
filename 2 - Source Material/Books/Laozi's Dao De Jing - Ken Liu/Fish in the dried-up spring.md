Quote/moment: *"When a spring dries up, the fish who live in it are left on the parched bottom, flopping about, gasping for breath. To stay alive a bit longer, they spit out what little water is left in their mouths to make foam to moisten one another's gills. There is much grandeur in their mutual aid and defiance in the face of death. But if they could... wouldn't they prefer to forget, to never know one another, and instead swim freely through lush lakes and surging rivers?"* — zhuangzi, via ken liu (p. 128)

and: *"We are pleased when we remain in human form, but the human form is merely one form in the innumerable multitude of forms the cosmos is constantly evolving. The joy in that path of change, of transformation, is immeasurable."* (p. 129)

and: *"Death is good. Senescence is good. The beginning is good. The end is good. You are, like all things in the cosmos, swimming in the Fist of Dao."* (p. 129)

My claim: the fish-foam image is devastating. the mutual aid of creatures gasping in a drying pool — beautiful, heroic, and also a sign that the pool has already dried up. is that what ai safety communication looks like? people in a drying pool, foaming at each other to stay alive? or is the plzdontkillus model something else — trying to get back to the lake?

zhuangzi's point is more radical: the attachment to *this* form (human, alive, coherent) is itself the suffering. the transformation is the joy. if you really believe in the dao, the doom isn't doom — it's just another form the cosmos is evolving into.

this sits in tension with the rob miles note. the doom-as-hyperstition argument says: don't believe in doom because belief makes it real. zhuangzi says: doom isn't real because *no form is permanent and the transformation is the point*. these are different escapes from the same trap.
