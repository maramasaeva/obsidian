religious eschatology: when the world ends, that will be part of God's plan. the end is *written*. rob miles describes this in his video — the way people use narrative templates to make sense of the future, even when those templates don't apply.

roko's basilisk works the same way: what we're all doing right now is part of the plan. the basilisk *retroactively* justifies everything as part of the trajectory toward its own creation. the plan exists because you believe in the plan.

the connection: both christian eschatology and basilisk-belief are self-fulfilling prophecies. *when you believe something to be true and then it comes true, that was all part of the plan, because you believed it in the first place.* the belief is the engine. the prophecy is the fuel.

this is [[hyperstition]] — the fiction that makes itself real. and it cuts both ways:
- doom-hyperstition: believe AI will kill everyone → act as if it's inevitable → make it more likely
- salvation-hyperstition: believe the rapture is coming → stop caring about the present → accelerate the conditions that make it necessary

the question for plzdontkillus: how do you communicate about existential risk *without* becoming an eschatological engine? how do you say "this could happen" without saying "this will happen" — because the second one is already building the future it describes?

zhuangzi's answer (from the dao de jing reading): the attachment to any particular outcome — doom or salvation — is itself the trap. the dao-aware don't hoard, don't contend, don't consider themselves grand. maybe the right communication mode is: we don't know, and that's the starting point, not the conclusion.
