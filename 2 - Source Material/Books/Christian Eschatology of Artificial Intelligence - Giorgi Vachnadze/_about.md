# Christian Eschatology of Artificial Intelligence — Giorgi Vachnadze

**Author:** Giorgi Vachnadze
**Published:** 2024 (Becoming Press, Berlin/Nicosia)
**Shelf:** currently-reading
**Started:** 2026-06-15

---

vachnadze's central move: take two discursive formations that claim to be completely external to one another — christian-pastoral techniques of self-formation and technocratic-neoliberal technologies of the self — and show that they draw on the same forms of subjugation, produce the same types of subjects, generate the same docility. the book is not saying christianity caused AI or AI is secretly christian. it's saying both regimes of truth share a deep structure that neither wants to acknowledge.

heavily foucauldian. draws on the *confessions of the flesh* (foucault's 4th volume on history of sexuality), dreyfus's AI critique, stypinska on digital pastorate, carrière on thinking-as-dwelling, koopman on datafied personhood. the method is genealogical — not what AI *is*, but what historical conditions of power made it *thinkable*.

dedicated to achim szepanski.

### chapter two — music and the poetics of time (p. 69–90)

vachnadze pivots from the historical apparatus of christian self-governance (chapter one's diagram of the flesh) to its contemporary double: the quantified self. drawing on deborah lupton, the chapter maps self-tracking technologies — biosensors, fitness apps, mood diaries, fertility monitors — as a digital pastoral that operates through the same logic as monastic confessional practice. the key move: neither the ancient nor the digital pastorate *prohibits*. both produce a lifestyle, shower the subject with encouragement, and marginalise those who refuse to participate.

the parallels are drawn tight. the greco-roman *aphrodisia* aimed at autonomy through self-mastery; the christian diagram at self-renunciation and docility; the quantified self at productivity and *peak performance*. all three share the injunction of *gnothi seauton* — know thyself — but what counts as "knowledge" is radically different each time. nudging systems function as modern exagoreusis: positive, productive, directing conduct without explicit prohibition. self-tracking devices become *mobile confessionals* where data replaces the verbalization of sin, and algorithmic output replaces prayer and penance.

the chapter then turns to latour's actor-network theory to argue that technological artefacts are not neutral instruments but genuine actors. the subject is dispersed, "overtaken" — *the actor is the network*. the quantified self emerges as a hybrid: christian subject + homo oeconomicus + cybernetic subject, all reinforcing one another under the signs of god, capital, and AI.

the final third is where it gets interesting. vachnadze brings in deleuzian bergsonism to argue that temporality — lived duration, qualitative difference — is what resists the computational episteme's reduction of experience to spatial data points. philosophy becomes *an acid poured on everyday life*. wittgenstein's analysis of musical performance crystallises the argument: mechanically reproducing a score is not understanding the music. the "voices from within" that emerge only when you listen — that is the kind of self-knowledge no apparatus can capture. NLP software and chatGPT are then named as *the automated confessional par excellence*: we have become the unpaid labour compiling our own psychological profiles.

**key concepts**: quantified self, nudging as exagoreusis, mobile confessional, actor-network theory, cybernetic flesh, bergsonian duration vs. spatial reduction, wittgenstein on music

### chapter three — the incomputability of computation (p. 91–108)

this is the most philosophically dense chapter so far. vachnadze stages the wittgenstein-turing debate through stuart shanker's reading and pushes it toward a foucauldian genealogy of AI.

wittgenstein's challenge: asking *can a machine think?* is not an empirical question about technological progress. it is a category mistake — like asking *has the number 3 a colour?* the question itself *bewitches* us, a picture that holds thought captive. turing's "bridging argument" tried to move from mathematical logic into the philosophy of mind via *on computable numbers*, but wittgenstein catches the sleight of hand. the phrases "so to speak" and "directly aware" in turing's text smuggle in philosophical presuppositions about mental states that have no empirical or analytical backing.

the central reversal: where turing says humans are machines that calculate, wittgenstein flips it — *turing machines are really humans who calculate*. machines are humans who *only* calculate, without thinking. this is not a limitation claim about current technology. it is a grammatical point about what "thinking" and "calculating" mean.

vachnadze then extends this through wittgenstein's *remarks on the foundations of mathematics*: calculation is a *normative social practice*, embedded in a lifeworld (*lebensform*). rule-following requires justification, explanation, correction — practices that fall outside the computational model. the mapping of a rule onto behaviour does not entail that the rule is being *followed*. this amounts to what vachnadze calls an *archaeology of proofs* — a genealogical uncovering of the *savoir* of machine learning, the cultural norms and practices that underlie and support the *connaissance* (surface effect) of algorithms. or as he puts it more bluntly: for the last 60-70 years, basic linear algebra has been rebranded and marketed as human intelligence.

gödel's incompleteness theorem runs parallel: if formal systems are turing-computable, then incompleteness applies to software programs too. the fundamental question is not whether machines calculate well, but whether they can *participate in non-formalizable, embodied and spontaneous human practices that constitute the savoir of our life*.

the chapter's sharpest claim: turing's formulation of computability is *the biblical canon of artificial intelligence*. the attempt to render rules "meaningless" and "neutral" is itself a theological operation — a neo-platonic delusion. the underlying political agenda: replace normative human behaviour with mechanical devices so that "responsibility gaps" protect those who benefit from the machine's inability to question its own actions. the discourse on AI mirrors religious rhetoric exactly — god as either the unfathomable black box or as intelligent design (the rational clockwork universe).

the chapter closes with a speculative opening: could a deleuzian reading of wittgenstein produce a "deleuze-computable" ontology? rhizomatic families of robots, algorithmic plateaus of immanence, machinic language-games — a flat ontology where everything is a machine, but nothing like a turing machine. left unresolved.

**key concepts**: category mistake, mechanist thesis, bridging argument, calculation as normative practice, archaeology of proofs, savoir/connaissance, theologico-machinic unconscious, gödel and incompleteness, deleuze-computable
