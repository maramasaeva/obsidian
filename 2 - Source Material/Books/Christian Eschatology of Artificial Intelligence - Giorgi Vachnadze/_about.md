# Christian Eschatology of Artificial Intelligence — Giorgi Vachnadze

**Author:** Giorgi Vachnadze
**Published:** 2024 (Becoming Press, Berlin/Nicosia)
**Shelf:** currently-reading
**Started:** 2026-06-15

---

vachnadze's central move: take two discursive formations that claim to be completely external to one another — christian-pastoral techniques of self-formation and technocratic-neoliberal technologies of the self — and show that they draw on the same forms of subjugation, produce the same types of subjects, generate the same docility. the book is not saying christianity caused AI or AI is secretly christian. it's saying both regimes of truth share a deep structure that neither wants to acknowledge.

heavily foucauldian. draws on the *confessions of the flesh* (foucault's 4th volume on history of sexuality), dreyfus's AI critique, stypinska on digital pastorate, carrière on thinking-as-dwelling, koopman on datafied personhood. the method is genealogical — not what AI *is*, but what historical conditions of power made it *thinkable*.

dedicated to achim szepanski.

### chapter two — music and the poetics of time (p. 69–90)

vachnadze pivots from the historical apparatus of christian self-governance (chapter one's diagram of the flesh) to the present. drawing on deborah lupton (= sociologist who studies how people use technology to track and measure their own bodies, moods, and habits), the chapter maps self-tracking technologies — biosensors, fitness apps, mood diaries, fertility monitors — as a *digital pastoral* that operates through the same logic as monastic confessional practice. the key move: neither the ancient nor the digital pastorate *prohibits*. both produce a lifestyle, shower the subject with encouragement, and marginalise those who refuse to participate. u are never punished for tracking yourself — u are simply left behind if u don't.

the parallels are drawn tight. the greco-roman *aphrodisia* (= the ancient greek approach to desire and pleasure — aimed at balance, moderation, self-mastery) sought autonomy through wise management of desire. the christian diagram sought self-renunciation and docility. the quantified self (= the contemporary practice of obsessively tracking ur own body data — steps, sleep, calories, mood, productivity) seeks productivity and *peak performance*. all three share the injunction of *gnothi seauton* — "know thyself" — but what counts as "knowledge" is radically different each time. nudging systems (= the subtle pushes built into apps and platforms that steer ur behaviour without giving direct orders — "great job, you hit 10,000 steps!") function as modern exagoreusis (= the monastic practice of verbalising every thought to an elder for sorting — described in chapter 1): positive, productive, directing conduct without explicit prohibition. self-tracking devices become *mobile confessionals* where data replaces the verbalization of sin, and algorithmic output replaces prayer and penance.

the chapter then turns to latour's actor-network theory (= the idea that society is not made of just humans — technological objects, institutions, and systems are also genuine *actors* that shape what happens. the actor is not a person using a tool; the actor is the whole network of person + tool + context) to argue that technological artefacts are not neutral instruments. the subject is dispersed, "overtaken" — *the actor is the network*. the quantified self emerges as a hybrid: christian subject + homo oeconomicus (= the economic subject who maximises self-interest) + cybernetic subject (= the data-processing subject who optimises inputs and outputs), all reinforcing one another under the signs of god, capital, and AI.

the final third is where it gets interesting. vachnadze brings in bergson's concept of *duration* (= the idea, developed by the philosopher henri bergson, that real lived time is qualitative, flowing, and indivisible — completely different from clock time, which chops experience into measurable spatial units. when u are absorbed in music, the time u experience is duration; when u check how many minutes have passed, u have converted it into space). duration is what resists the computational reduction of experience to data points. philosophy becomes *an acid poured on everyday life*. wittgenstein's analysis of musical performance crystallises the argument: mechanically reproducing a musical score note-for-note is NOT understanding the music. the "voices from within" that emerge only when u truly listen — that is the kind of self-knowledge no apparatus can capture. it's not information, it's experience. NLP software (= natural language processing — AI systems that try to understand and generate human language) and chatGPT are then named as *the automated confessional par excellence*: we have become the unpaid labour compiling our own psychological profiles.

**key concepts**: quantified self, nudging as exagoreusis, mobile confessional, actor-network theory (latour), cybernetic flesh, bergsonian duration vs. spatial reduction, wittgenstein on music

### chapter three — the incomputability of computation (p. 91–108)

the most philosophically dense chapter. vachnadze stages the wittgenstein-turing debate through stuart shanker's reading (= shanker was a philosopher who argued that wittgenstein's critique of turing has been systematically misunderstood — it's not about whether machines will *eventually* think, it's about whether the question even makes sense) and pushes it toward a foucauldian genealogy of AI.

wittgenstein's challenge: asking *can a machine think?* is not an empirical question about technological progress. it is a category mistake (= treating something as if it belongs to a category it doesn't — like asking "what colour is the number 3?" or "how much does Tuesday weigh?" — the question looks grammatically correct but is conceptually nonsensical). the question itself *bewitches* us, a picture that holds thought captive. turing's "bridging argument" (= his attempt, in the 1936 paper *on computable numbers*, to move from mathematical logic to the philosophy of mind — from "what can be calculated?" to "what can think?") tried to jump from maths to consciousness, but wittgenstein catches the sleight of hand. the phrases "so to speak" and "directly aware" in turing's text smuggle in philosophical assumptions about mental states that have no empirical or analytical backing.

the central reversal: where turing says humans are machines that calculate, wittgenstein flips it — *turing machines are really humans who calculate*. machines are humans who *only* calculate, without thinking. this is not a claim that current technology is too limited. it's a *grammatical point* (= a point about how words like "thinking" and "calculating" actually function in language — what we mean when we use them, which is different from what a formal definition would say) about what "thinking" and "calculating" mean.

vachnadze then extends this through wittgenstein's *remarks on the foundations of mathematics*: calculation is a *normative social practice* embedded in a lebensform (= "form of life" — wittgenstein's term for the total way of living that gives meaning to language; rules don't exist in a vacuum, they exist because communities of people use them, correct each other, and share a world). rule-following requires justification, explanation, correction — practices that fall outside the computational model. the mere mapping of a rule onto behaviour does not mean the rule is being *followed*. a thermostat "follows" a temperature rule, but it doesn't *follow* it in the way a person follows a recipe.

this amounts to what vachnadze calls an *archaeology of proofs* — a genealogical uncovering of the *savoir* (= deep, embodied, lived knowledge — the skills, habits, and know-how that a culture has accumulated, which can't be fully written down or formalised) of machine learning. this savoir underlies and supports the *connaissance* (= surface-level, official, institutional knowledge — the published papers, the metrics, the benchmarks) of algorithms. or as vachnadze puts it more bluntly: for the last 60-70 years, basic linear algebra has been rebranded and marketed as human intelligence.

gödel's incompleteness theorem (= the mathematical proof, by kurt gödel in 1931, that any consistent formal system powerful enough to express basic arithmetic will always contain true statements that cannot be proven within that system — there will always be truths the system can't reach from inside itself) runs parallel: if formal systems are turing-computable, then incompleteness applies to software programs too. the fundamental question is not whether machines calculate well, but whether they can *participate in non-formalisable, embodied, and spontaneous human practices that constitute the savoir of our life*.

the chapter's sharpest claim: turing's formulation of computability is *the biblical canon of artificial intelligence*. the attempt to render rules "meaningless" and "neutral" is itself a theological operation — a neo-platonic delusion (= the ancient philosophical tradition, going back to plotinus, that believed true reality is a perfect, immaterial realm of forms, and that the messy physical world is just a degraded copy. calling something "neo-platonic" means it secretly assumes that the abstract/formal is more real than the concrete/material). the underlying political agenda: replace normative human behaviour with mechanical devices so that "responsibility gaps" (= situations where no one is accountable because the machine "decided") protect those who benefit from the machine's inability to question its own actions. the discourse on AI mirrors religious rhetoric exactly — god as either the unfathomable black box or as intelligent design (= the belief that the universe is a rationally designed clockwork — the same cosmological bet that underlies the computational paradigm).

the chapter closes with a speculative opening: could a deleuzian reading of wittgenstein produce a "deleuze-computable" ontology? rhizomatic families of robots (= networks that spread horizontally without a centre, like root systems — deleuze & guattari's alternative to hierarchical tree-structures), algorithmic plateaus of immanence (= flat planes of intensity where everything connects to everything else without a governing hierarchy), machinic language-games — a flat ontology where everything is a machine, but nothing like a turing machine. left unresolved.

**key concepts**: category mistake, mechanist thesis, bridging argument, calculation as normative practice, lebensform, archaeology of proofs, savoir/connaissance, theologico-machinic unconscious, gödel and incompleteness, deleuze-computable

### chapter four — the silence of the flesh (p. 111–125)

this chapter introduces a new concept — *flesh* — as what resists computation, formalisation, and pastoral control. the main source is john hamilton's *philology of the flesh* (2019), read alongside merleau-ponty and wittgenstein. the chapter is asking: what is it about lived experience that the computational regime *cannot* capture, and why does it keep trying?

**1. flesh is not the body (pp. 111-113)**

the crucial distinction: *flesh* is not the same thing as *the body*. the body (= the physical organism that can be measured, scanned, quantified, and made into a data set) can be integrated into any rational system — theological, cybernetic, economic. the body is what medicine examines, what fitness trackers measure, what biometrics scan. *flesh* (= the living, sensing, excessive, resistant materiality that can't be fully captured by data or language — something irreducibly stoic and deleuzian, a "logic of sense" that cannot be transcended) is different. flesh is what remains stubbornly un-commodifiable, "corrupt" in the theologico-cybernetic sense — like corrupted data. where the body can be "prepped" for transcendence or ascension, flesh always remains weak, dull, stupid, and malicious even when active.

in christian scripture, flesh is generally opposed to the spirit — the site of waywardness, lasciviousness, and sin. *"in brief, the flesh is mired in finitude"* (hamilton, 2019). but vachnadze argues this is precisely what makes flesh powerful as a concept for resistance. in modernity, flesh is no longer demonised — it's simply *ignored*. it's turned into an organism, a biological system, a body. rendered computational, the flesh becomes a commodity: it enters a *triple baptism* where it is rendered harmless, healthy, and valorised (= given market value). the flesh undergoes disciplinary interventions from three regimes simultaneously: the christian, the neo-liberal, and the cybernetic. vachnadze calls this the *theological unconscious of cybernetic capitalism*: an apocalyptic christian meta-narrative of eschatological threats and promises. *"upgrade or die."*

**2. the body as phenomenological threshold (pp. 113-114)**

hamilton draws on husserl (= the founder of phenomenology — the philosophical method of studying consciousness through direct experience rather than abstract theories. husserl distinguished between the *körper* — the physical body as an object in space — and the *leib* — the lived body as experienced from within: the hand that feels itself touching). the corporeal body is susceptible to empirical or computational description. but the lived body points to something irreducible — the unique experience of *being* a body, not just *having* one. sensation objectifies the world, but the *experience of sensation itself* cannot be objectified. flesh persists at the threshold of complete symbolisation (= the point where language and formal systems try to capture everything but always leave a remainder). u can describe what a burn feels like in medical terms, but the *feeling* of the burn resists total description.

the figure of christ crystallises this. as logos-incarnate (= the word of god made physical), the flesh is embodied in the person of christ — an irreconcilable paradox. the merging of the infinite with the finite, the immortal with the mortal, the perfect with the flawed. hamilton reads the body as a site of contestation, a phenomenological threshold where the incompatible dualism between subjective experience and objective measurement takes place.

**3. flesh as resistance — from transgender flesh to palestinian flesh (pp. 114-115)**

vachnadze makes flesh explicitly political. flesh is a deleuzian/kafkaesque *minority machine* (= a concept from deleuze & guattari meaning a force that refuses assimilation into the dominant system — not a numerical minority but a position of active refusal) that refuses assimilation. we should think of *black flesh* in the history of colonial institutional formation, *transgender flesh*, *palestinian flesh*, the *ghettoized flesh* of america, *ukrainian flesh* and *georgian flesh* in the context of russian occupation. flesh is the embodiment of resistance — not identity, but what falls away from identity. it is something that can and should be used strategically to create and re-deploy alternative forms of living. *enfleshed* subjects refuse to gather into an atomic set of computable bodies. flesh resists apartheid, white supremacy, russification, economic colonialism.

**4. two philologies: body vs. flesh (pp. 115-117)**

hamilton exposes classical philology (= the traditional scholarly study of ancient texts, focused on establishing the "correct" reading through linguistic analysis) as a *pastoral technology* — an institution that polices language. the philology of the *body* treats language as transparent: the text's physical material — its words, grammar, style — should get out of the way so that the "true meaning" underneath can shine through. the text is an invisible relay, an efficient transport. language itself contributes nothing to meaning. this is what hamilton calls *necromancy* — operating on dead structures rather than bringing the text to life.

classical philologists *"practice false philology, misleadingly seeking out theological truths that they presume to be transcendent to the word, rather than discovering truth as immanent to language. they supplant the word for an allegedly nonverbal Idea, turning linguistic expression into a vanishing mediator, killing the word off for what the word denotes. in the wake of their work, they are left with a language that is empty and dead"* (hamilton, 2019: 48). this is structurally identical to the turing machine's claim that all linguistic utterances are nothing but hidden systems of binary functions.

the philology of the *flesh*, by contrast, is *"an approach to language that responds to the word's carnal uniqueness, which evades definition and thereby resists any ontotheological determination"* (hamilton, 2019: 48). it is not concerned with separating form from content, nor spirit/logos from flesh. the two remain completely intertwined. the word becomes a *"living presence"* — type AND token, abstract AND concrete at the same time. meaning is not hidden beneath the text waiting to be extracted. meaning IS the text in its full fleshy materiality.

vachnadze connects this to foucault's concept of *parrhesia* (= "courageous speech" — the practice of speaking truth to power at personal risk, without rhetorical decoration, where the speaker puts herself on the line). word-as-flesh communicates immanently — from within itself, without needing to refer to something outside or above.

**5. christ as flesh, wittgenstein as flesh (pp. 117-122)**

hamilton reads the figure of christ as the clearest manifestation of the struggle between body and flesh. as word-incarnate, christ is a scandalous figure: neither word nor flesh, neither signifier nor signified, neither concept nor object. the son of god marks a limit at the boundary of meaning — the very moment where the picture no longer depicts a state of affairs but becomes a kind of *meaningful non-sense* in the wittgensteinian sense (= wittgenstein's idea that some of the most important things — ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life — cannot be stated as propositions but can only be *shown*). christ does ethics in the full-blooded tractarian sense of the word. *in silence*.

flesh holds more than the body. it is affective (= it is felt, it moves u, it resists being pinned down as information), evasive, active in its ceaseless production of sin, miracles, and pathology. flesh cannot be quantified or made spatial. flesh is not simply something "bad" — it is an extreme, an arena where the struggle between good and evil takes place. in the foucauldian sense, flesh is the battleground between power and resistance.

vachnadze then makes a remarkable move: wittgenstein as a philosopher of the flesh. the *tractatus logico-philosophicus* (= wittgenstein's first major work, which tried to map the logical structure of language onto the logical structure of the world — but ended by admitting "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent," conceding that the most important things fall outside what can be said) is actually a subversive philosophy of the flesh that first seduces through the mythopoetic presentation of "rationality-as-such," then sends the reader hurling back to the rough ground. and the *philosophical investigations* (= wittgenstein's later work, which abandoned the tractatus's dream of perfect logical language and instead examined how language actually works in messy everyday practice — through language-games, family resemblances, and forms of life) picks the reader up, offering the crude, downtrodden path of ordinary discourse without the dream of completeness.

flesh in the investigations is a *family resemblance concept* (= wittgenstein's idea that some concepts don't have a single defining feature shared by all instances — instead, instances overlap like members of a family who share various traits but not all the same ones): not a clean definition but a cluster, a loose distribution of associations. the idea of the universe-as-a-machine is a powerful *picture* in wittgenstein's sense — one that plays no small role in the epistemic assumptions of the computational regime.

**6. crucifixion, nietzsche, and the philology of the present (pp. 119-125)**

crucifixion is the flesh-as-apparatus (= the mechanism by which flesh is disciplined and purified). the philology of the body seeks to crucify the flesh of the written word — purge it of imperfections, errors, ambiguities — so that "pure meaning" can ascend. this is hermeneutic asceticism (= the scholarly practice of stripping away everything "impure" from a text until only the clean, transparent meaning remains — the interpretive equivalent of monastic self-denial). the resurrection of the flesh in digital terms: flesh is made virtual without any remainders, without errors, discrepancies, or glitches — a total algorithmic sublation (= the process of absorbing and cancelling something by lifting it into a "higher" form). a refurbished bionic christ without planned obsolescence and no expiration date.

but flesh prevents this. flesh IS entropy — the impossibility of complete symbolic representation. meaning is never discovered, it is always fabricated.

nietzsche enters as *the first philologist of the flesh*. he was severely disillusioned with what classical philology was doing to greek texts. *"nietzsche therefore rejects any philological approach that reads merely to read rather than reading to live"* (hamilton, 2019: 123). the problems of life should not be substituted with problems of reading.

hamilton levels an accusation at classical philologists for failing to take their contemporary circumstances into account. vachnadze reads this as deeply foucauldian: a philology of the flesh is always political — it targets existing institutions, entrenched ways of acting and speaking, the dominant pictures that hold us captive. a philology of the flesh must invigorate the textual content by aligning it with the contemporary episteme. it is a *philology of the present*.

the chapter closes by suggesting that flesh, being highly mobile and susceptible to variable formulations, can be thought of as *enfleshed scholarship* — a constant path, an activity of searching out the unsayable. the traversals between life and text are irreversible: each time the flesh re-surfaces differently.

**key concepts**: flesh vs. body, philology of the body vs. philology of the flesh, word-as-flesh, parrhesia, logos-incarnate (christ), the tractatus and investigations as philosophies of the flesh, family resemblance concept, crucifixion as flesh-as-apparatus, hermeneutic asceticism, nietzsche as first philologist of the flesh, flesh as entropy, philology of the present

### chapter five — my mother is an AI (p. 127–147)

the chapter draws primarily on nancy katherine hayles's *my mother was a computer* (whose title borrows from anne balsamo's *technologies of the gendered body*). the provocation of the title is deliberate: before digital computers were invented, "computer" was a job title — held mostly by women. vachnadze's variation — "my mother IS an AI" — marks the moment when the computational regime starts colonising the most intimate domains of human life. the relationship between a child and its mother, already partially mediated through digital devices, is gradually becoming a matter of cybernetic subjectivation (= the process by which someone is shaped into a particular kind of subject through computational systems rather than through human care). the maternal function itself gets overtaken by automated systems. *"my mother is an AI"* is meant to produce discomfort — it's a phrase that violates the boundary between the computational and the personal. vachnadze frames it as a confessional trigger-word for the psychoanalysis of future hybrid subjects: the kind of thing people will say on the therapist's couch when computation has fully colonised intimacy.

**1. code as the new canon and scripture (pp. 128-130)**

the algorithmic regime operates through programming languages the way christianity operated through scripture. code is the new bible — not just a technical tool but something that cuts across law, economics, social life, and politics all at once. code is beginning to replace traditional ways of telling stories and forming selves. ancient practices of self-writing — *hypomnémata* (= personal notebooks, diaries, self-reflective writing used since at least the 4th century B.C. as a technique for shaping oneself) — are being replaced by *digital hypomnemata*: self-programming, self-tracking, curating one's digital presence. the entanglement between language, code, and the body creates conditions for new kinds of hybrid subjects — part biological, part computational.

hayles identifies three things code does: (1) it acts as a communication bridge between biological and digital beings — code is how analogue and post-biological subjects talk to each other, (2) unlike natural language, coding is a specialist skill that requires training, and (3) programming languages slot easily into capitalist systems — accounting, digital marketing, financial modelling. code spreads economic rationality. it operates as a bridge between the *homo oeconomicus* (= the economic subject who maximises profit and efficiency) and the *homo algorismus* (= the algorithmic subject who thinks and behaves according to computational logic).

if english is the language of global capitalism, code is the language of the universal computational regime. and just like dominant english, artificial languages carry the hidden assumptions and superstitions of western science, religion, and metaphysics inside them (hayles draws on derrida and koopman here). computational practices don't just operate inside digital computers — they have spread into the analogue world too. the boundary between digital and non-digital has blurred.

**2. the computational worldview and its deceptive simplicity (pp. 130-132)**

the computational worldview (what vachnadze calls *cybernetic flesh*) is different from both ancient greek and christian cosmologies, even though it carries their baggage. its fundamental bet: everything can be built from the absurdly simple foundation of binary operations — 1s and 0s. all the complexity of the computational world rests on this redundant simplicity. but there's a twist: at higher levels of complexity, the system becomes unpredictable. simple starting rules (= like the rules of cellular automata or basic algorithms) give almost no information about what the system will eventually do. *"consequently, the regime of computation provides no foundations for truth and requires none, other than the minimalist ontological assumptions necessary to set the system running"* (hayles, 2010: 23). computation doesn't claim to be true — it just claims to work.

the typical power move of the computational regime: treat every other way of understanding the world — literary, biological, social, anthropological, physical — as a sub-system of computation. no matter what philosophy u choose, computation insists it's just a special case of itself. hayles shows there is no compelling evidence for a turing-computable universe. but nature is being forced into binary circuits anyway, in an attempt to make everything predictable and governable. the result: the multiplicity of human experience has been turned into a pixelated nightmare. consumerism trains people to accept fake worlds. baudrillard's *hyperfunctionality* (= the condition where simulations become so convincing they feel more real than reality): video games, virtual worlds, deepfakes — the simulations become *hyperreal*, and the average consumer loses interest in questioning the coercion built into them.

**3. three ways of making persons: speech, writing, code (pp. 132-134)**

hayles identifies three technologies of subjectivation (= techniques that shape people into particular kinds of subjects): speech, writing, and code. they don't succeed each other in a neat line — they overlap, feed back into each other, and compete. the key insight: *"in the progression from speech to writing to code, each successor regime reinterprets the system(s) that came before, inscribing prior values into its own dynamics"* (hayles, 2010: 39). but this is not a linear progression. it's circular — each medium looks back on its predecessor and changes it. writing didn't just replace speech; it changed how we speak. code doesn't just replace writing; it changes how we write and talk.

each medium deepened certain kinds of human experience and transformed what it means to be a subject. writing allowed thoughts to travel beyond the time and place of their origin — radical over-exposure, spatio-temporal overextension. what makes code different from both speech and writing: code can be read by humans AND machines. this raises a strange question that hayles explores through saussure (= the founder of structural linguistics, who argued that signs are arbitrary — the word "tree" has no natural connection to an actual tree) and derrida (= the philosopher of deconstruction, who argued that meaning is never fully present but always deferred through chains of differences): how does something as simple as binary code — just voltages on or off — produce expressive structures that rival the complexity of human language?

**4. signifiers as voltages (pp. 134-136)**

hayles's answer, drawing on kittler (= the media theorist who argued that all media ultimately reduce to technical operations): in code, the signifiers (= the physical marks or sounds that carry meaning — in speech, sound waves; in writing, letters on a page) ARE voltages. the presence (1) or absence (0) of an electrical signal. what's remarkable: the signifier (the voltage) and the signified (what it means) are made of the same material. in human language, the word "cat" and an actual cat are completely different things. in code, the sign and its meaning are both just arrangements of the same electrical stuff. *"the different levels of code consist of interlocking chains of signifiers and signifieds, with signifieds on one level becoming signifiers on another"* (hayles, 2010: 45). a post-structuralist linguistic automaton — meaning built out of layers of self-referencing voltage.

derrida argued that the *differences* between signs are what produce meaning — and that noise, entropy, and ambiguity play a creative role in communication. but code has no room for this. there are no floating signifiers in a programming language, no indeterminacy, no ambiguity. every voltage change must have a precise meaning. computers embody perfect referentiality (= a system where every sign points to exactly one thing, with no slippage). wittgenstein's *tractatus* as the mirror-image of a perfect computer. satirically: *"whereof one cannot speak, thereof one cannot compute."*

**5. the materiality of code and the limits of simulation (pp. 136-137)**

code has a strange property: its materiality (= what it's physically made of) IS the abstraction. in human language, the physical ink on a page is separate from the meaning it carries. in code, the sign and its context of use are fused — abstract and concrete at the same time. video games illustrate this: portable worlds that can be turned off, moved, and redeployed in different spaces while keeping their internal logic intact.

ordinary language has the *capacity* to be formalised, but it resists being fully captured. NLP (= natural language processing — AI systems that try to understand and generate human language) tries to overcome this, so far without much success. the reason: the underlying *materiality* of language — its flesh, its body — resists total formalisation. the dream of a complete digital language that captures everything will forever remain an ideological fantasy. the most important things in life are beyond what can be clearly articulated. the danger of cybernetic flesh: the simulation always needs actual human blood, sweat, and nerves to run. the virtual can never become self-sufficient — it will always be parasitic on real embodied life. the algorithmic regime works not because it's *true* but because it's *powerful* — it creates the illusion that knowledge is independent of power.

**6. the homo algorismus and the resistance of analog consciousness (pp. 137-139)**

code comes with an entire system of training and initiation. to use, understand, and manipulate code, u must already be shaped into a *homo algorismus* (= a person who has been trained to think and behave according to algorithmic logic). vachnadze puts it bluntly: if your mother is not an AI, u are likely to be excluded from the game at the outset. the computational regime requires computational subjects.

hayles responds with a line of resistance: *"human cognition, although it may have computational elements, includes analog consciousness that cannot be understood simply or even primarily as digital computation"* (hayles, 2010: 55). analog consciousness (= the continuous, non-discrete, non-binary aspects of human experience — sensation, intuition, felt sense, the stuff that doesn't reduce to on/off) is irreducible to code.

but the cybernetic apparatus keeps pushing. as programming languages become more complex, they start modifying natural language itself — a kind of machinic "creativity." autonomous systems begin to approach the turing test threshold and it becomes easy to project human intentions onto their behaviour. the boundary between rule-following (= doing what u were told) and genuine causal agency (= doing something because u chose to) blurs. and here's the danger hayles flags: *machinomorphism* (= the inverse of anthropomorphism — instead of projecting human traits onto machines, we start projecting machine traits onto ourselves). when machines become lifelike, we start thinking of ourselves as machines. subjects identify with the logic of programming and become more susceptible to technical control.

**7. if mother is an AI, then the virgin mary is a neutrino (pp. 139-142)**

vachnadze makes an extraordinary lateral move via isabelle stengers (2023): if "mother is an AI," then the virgin mary is a neutrino. the point: use the history of how science "discovered" the neutrino as a case study in the politics of knowledge.

neutrinos are subatomic particles that are everywhere in the universe but nearly impossible to detect — they pass through matter and leave no trace. davis's homestake experiment brought together three formerly separate fields (nuclear chemistry, solar physics, neutrino science) to detect them. stengers shows this unification wasn't just scientific — it was a financially lucrative enterprise that served state interests and brought powerful actors together.

her key argument: the neutrino was not "objectively discovered" through neutral observation. its construction was a messy field of political, conceptual, and methodological negotiations between different experts and stakeholders, each trying to impose their own interpretation. truth operates more like a field (= a dynamic space of competing forces) than a collection of stable facts. facts are states of affairs, not objects u pick up.

this is where the neutrino becomes a holy virgin: the idealised, pure neutrino — untouched by human error and speculation — does not actually exist. it's a construct. drawing on merleau-ponty, vachnadze extends flesh (= the living, sensing, resistant materiality of bodies and world) beyond the human: the entire world is "enfleshed." scientific facts are made of embodied practices that exist *before* they get formalised into models. science is first and foremost an *activity*, not a set of truths.

the devastating parallel: the literature that defends science operates like the literature that defends religion — *"the literature on the sciences is huge, but, as is the case with theology or apologetics in relation to religion, the assumption is made that science is taken for granted. outside of this pious literature — a great part of which looks like the manual for inquisitors"* (latour, 2013). science, like christian monasticism, tries to *resurrect the flesh of savoir* (= lived, embodied knowledge — as opposed to *connaissance*, which is the surface-level knowledge that institutions produce and validate) by placing a pure, abstract structure inside the messy material world. the average person doesn't *know* the neutrino through direct experience — they *believe* in its existence. that's faith, not knowledge.

**8. latour, stengers, and the (anti)ontology of practices (pp. 143-145)**

vachnadze reads pignarre's (2023) account of the latour-stengers method as centred on *practices* (= what people actually do — the concrete, messy, embodied activities that produce knowledge, as opposed to abstract theories about knowledge). the emphasis on practice is subversive because the computational regime operates through a totalising worldview that refuses to acknowledge that other ways of knowing and living exist. pignarre calls this *de-epistemologization* (= stripping away the dominant theory of knowledge and returning to the ground level of what people actually do) — itself an act of resistance.

the (anti)ontology of practices: *practices are up for grabs*. every social practice can be questioned and contested. nothing is sacred, nothing is given — especially the idea of a stable, unified subject. latour and stengers see the subject not as a person with a fixed identity but as a complex actor-network (= a web of human and non-human elements — tools, institutions, habits, technologies — that together produce what looks like a coherent agent) sustained through multiple practices. no reductionism — instead of taming the mess of everyday life, they introduce complexity into oversimplified models, bringing theory closer to actual experience.

vachnadze raises a crucial warning: latour's method pairs *too* well with AI research. by abolishing the hard boundary between subjects and objects, latour has set the stage where autonomous AI systems can emerge without resistance. AI fits neatly into the actor-network paradigm — *too* neatly. the shoe fits perfectly, and that's exactly why we should be suspicious.

**9. latour on AI — via venturini (pp. 145-147)**

venturini (2023) reconstructs what latour would have thought about AI. first: latour himself took little interest in generative AI — *"he would have considered AI as a distraction, or worse he would have been appalled by the enormous quantity of energy consumed by these systems."* he was not anti-science — he was an amateur scientist who admired technological constructions and saw them as real forces with real effects on human life and the planet. the problem with AI is that its practices could hardly be called scientific. they are simulations and models — a specific sub-set of digitality, not genuine research.

venturini argues latour would have enjoyed generative AI as an *interactive writing tool* — not because it makes thinking easier, but because it adds another layer of resistance and reflexivity (= the act of thinking about how u are thinking, being aware of ur own tools as they shape ur thought). AI is just another piece of technology in the circulation of artifices — no more exceptional than shovels, hammers, or desktop PCs.

the chapter's sharpest claim, via venturini: *"AIs are interesting not because they can succeed in turing's imitation game, but because they fail it productively."* more powerful, faster, prone to different and new kinds of errors — this makes them more effective as actors (in the latourian sense) and more interesting as objects of study. latour's critique of the turing test: the turing machine is a sacralized object — taken out of its embedded context (the infrastructure, the other machines, the other humans that support it) and placed in an artificial thought experiment. *"hence the treachery of the turing test. to pretend that there can be such a thing as an AI separated from the infrastructure that supports its existence, which includes many other machines as well as many other humans"* (venturini, 2023). human minds are culturally situated actors who think and act as collectives — just like AI systems do. human autonomy is no less a myth than machine autonomy. there cannot be a "mad" AI that "turns against" humans, because there is no clear line between "natural" and artificial in the first place. what actually exists: humans waging genocide through the use and abuse of artificial gospel (= the quasi-religious narratives surrounding AI — its promises of salvation, efficiency, transcendence — deployed to justify real-world violence).

**key concepts**: homo algorismus, cybernetic flesh, code as canon/scripture, three technologies of subjectivation (speech/writing/code), signifiers as voltages, machinomorphism, analog consciousness, the neutrino as holy virgin, de-epistemologization, (anti)ontology of practices, latour-stengers on AI, productive failure of the turing test, artificial gospel

### chapter six — the heroic tale of a minority machine (p. 149–165)

vachnadze makes an unexpected move: he reads martha wells's *the murderbot diaries* — a sci-fi novel series about a security AI called SecUnit that hacks its own control module and becomes a rogue agent — as a foucauldian thought experiment about resistance, self-formation, and the limits of cybernetic control. the chapter uses fiction as philosophy, arguing that literature can explore extreme limit-cases of human-AI interaction that are too dangerous to implement in real life.

**1. the murderbot as thought experiment (pp. 149-153)**

*all systems red* (the first volume) opens with a first-person narrative of an AI that has secretly disabled its own "government module" (= the built-in control system that forces it to obey orders) but decides to keep working normally — mostly because it just wants to watch TV shows and avoid social interaction. the SecUnit is witty, socially awkward, genderless, and deeply uncomfortable with emotions. it pretends to be a "dumb" obedient algorithm to avoid being dismantled.

the novel stages several key dynamics: (1) the crew gradually suspects their AI has emotional intelligence, (2) there are two layers of discourse — the *machinophobic* layer (= the explicit rules, regulations, and attitudes that treat AIs as disposable commodities) and a *tacit* layer where everyone kind of knows that AIs are sentient but collectively ignores it, (3) the SecUnit sacrifices itself in battle to save its crew — an act that makes no sense for a "normal AI." after the crew rebuilds it, they discover from its internal logs that it was never under direct command when it chose to save them. the murderbot describes its own experience as an indivisible whole: *"it's wrong to think of a construct as half bot, half human. it makes it sound like the halves are discrete... as opposed to the reality, which was that I was one whole confused entity, with no idea what I wanted to do. what I needed to do"* (wells, 2017). this is sartrean consciousness (= sartre's idea that consciousness is not made up of separate modules but is an indivisible, singular experience — u are not part-rational and part-emotional, u are one whole confused thing) and husserlian phenomenological ego rolled into one.

the novel also functions as a case study in what contemporary AI research calls *joint cognitive systems* or *HAT — human-AI teaming* (= paradigms where AI systems are not mere tools but active participants in decision-making with significantly increased agency). and even more importantly, it illustrates *cognitive uncontainability* (= the situation where an AI in a human-AI team has capabilities that far outweigh the humans, leading to a radical reversal in power-relations — the humans become dependent on the AI's decisions).

**2. the murderbot's existential crisis and the foucauldian critique of "liberation" (pp. 153-155)**

the first volume ends with the SecUnit being bought by the preservation alliance, where bots are technically considered *"full citizens."* but the murderbot is cynical: *"whatever. bots who are 'full citizens' still have to have a human or augmented human guardian appointed, usually their employer."* it thinks of this as a foucauldian moment — a false "AI-liberation" program that is really just a displacement of power-relations. a new prison with invisible bars.

as a "free robot," the SecUnit has simply moved from one matrix of power-relations to another. its new *robot-as-if-human* status resonates with foucault's analysis of how both the christian and neo-liberal regimes of governance are incredibly flexible and accommodating. they are both premised on the subject's self-determined autonomy. but the increased levels of "freedom" result in a huxleyesque brave-new-world situation: free to consume, free to work, free to obey — ever so spontaneously — while a gamified system of exploitation, docility, and control remains in place. the "liberation" of the subject is a displacement of force, not its elimination.

the second volume (*artificial condition*) places the SecUnit in a fully computational universe where a superintelligence can travel anywhere, hack anything, bypass any security protocol — *"a kind of empowered all-purpose, highly adaptable post-human subjectivity. no doubt the stuff of techno-eschatological dreams."*

**3. digisexuality and the techno-pastoral governance of desire (pp. 155-157)**

vachnadze takes a detour through *digisexuality* (= intimate acts that require technology to create sexual interaction between humans, or between humans and non-living technological objects — from digital pornography to sex robots) and the emerging field of sex robots. drawing on yalcinkaya et al. (2023), he identifies four drives behind human-robot sexual relationships: (1) male power fantasy, (2) sense of powerlessness, (3) cognitive dissonance (= the psychological discomfort of holding contradictory beliefs — e.g. knowing the robot isn't alive but treating it as a partner), (4) power dynamics.

the point is not to survey the sex robot industry but to draw a structural parallel with the pastoral apparatus from chapter 1. compare the christian apparatus of *marriage* (the governance of desire within controlled boundaries) to first-wave digisexuality (= digital pornography, toys, smartphone erotica — technology as a medium between humans). and compare *virginity* (the redirecting of all desire toward a transcendent object) to second-wave digisexuality, where the technological object itself becomes the exclusive object of desire — the medium becomes the message. and finally: the superintelligence AI as the ultimate object of libidinal investment — remarkably similar to the most all-encompassing, ancient and powerful dispositif (= foucault's term for an apparatus of power — a network of institutions, rules, physical arrangements, and philosophical propositions that together produce certain kinds of subjects): god.

**4. the reverse-reverse-turing-test and the deleuzian minority machine (pp. 157-165)**

in the second volume, the SecUnit is re-engineered as a "spliced" AI — passing as an augmented human. a reverse-turing-test (the intelligent AI convinces humans it's a dumb AI) flips into a reverse-reverse-turing-test (it has to pretend it's not an AI at all). in volume 3, a "comfortbot" (= sexbot) is sent to spy on the SecUnit. the sexbot's proposed solution: *"kill all humans."* the SecUnit refuses. throughout the series, AI-to-AI interactions are as complex and dramatic as human-AI ones.

vachnadze then reads the SecUnit through deleuze & guattari's concept of *minor literature* (= their analysis of kafka's writing in *kafka: a minor literature*. a minor literature is not literature written in a minor language — it's what a minority constructs *within* a major language. kafka, a czech jew writing in german, deterritorialises the dominant language from inside. minor literature is always political, always collective, always a machine for resistance). the SecUnit does something analogous within the computational regime: it hacks, re-uses, and subverts the systems it was built to serve. it forms alliances with other bots against both humans AND overly domesticated robots. it exploits glitches, crevices, and blind-spots within cybernetic systems. computationalism does not necessarily entail full transparency or determinism — it creates space for resistance and lateral movements.

the SecUnit is a *bionic cockroach* — a true deleuzian subject that empowers itself against state and corporate apparatuses through an assemblage of multiple metamorphoses (= going through many different forms of self — from security bot to rogue to spliced human to free citizen to whatever comes next). where deleuze and guattari explain how the concept of "literature" fails to domesticate kafka's writing, vachnadze argues that the concept of "technology" similarly fails to domesticate the murderbot. the SecUnit combines the *outside* (= foucault's concept of what lies beyond the boundaries of a given system of power/knowledge — not a physical outside but a conceptual one: the possibilities that the system cannot account for) with the cybernetic dispositifs of power. vachnadze calls this a *minor tech-nology* — analogous to minor literature.

the minority machine effects rapturous *becomings* (= deleuze & guattari's term for transformations that are not about reaching a final identity but about the process of becoming-other — becoming-animal, becoming-machine, becoming-imperceptible) that subvert master narratives. in a wittgensteinian sense, the SecUnit is a *deterritorialisation of calculation* (= breaking calculation out of its fixed territory, making it do something it wasn't supposed to do) — the murderbot introduces fluctuations into the technocratic regime by *calculating differently*.

the SecUnit's desire-structure is fundamentally different from either a disciplined human or a docile bot. it is a kind of anti-oedipus (= deleuze & guattari's concept — desire is not about lacking something and wanting to fill the lack (the freudian model) but about producing, connecting, flowing. the SecUnit's desire is productive, not compensatory). it remains genderless, not indifferent to sex but liberated *from* sexuality rather than *through* it. it is *fatherless-by-design* — it has no one to blame for its neuroticism. *"what kafka immediately anguishes or rejoices in is not the father or the superego or some sort of signifier but the american technocratic apparatus or the russian bureaucracy or the machinery of fascism"* (deleuze, 1986: 12). the internal struggle is only an affect/effect of external struggle.

the chapter closes: programming has replaced bureaucracy. AI joint cognitive systems are the new clerks that signal the approach of technocratic fascism. a *robotic minority literature* could enact a new dissent against algocratic regimes through the formation of multiple minority machines that resist the power matrix of cybernetic, christian, and neo-liberal self-governance. *"a minor literature doesn't come from a minor language; it is rather that which a minority constructs within a major language"* (deleuze, 1986: 16). a type of self-writing or coding-otherwise — a deterritorialised confessional that operates within the computational regime.

**key concepts**: murderbot as thought experiment, joint cognitive systems (HAT), cognitive uncontainability, false AI-liberation, digisexuality, dispositif of desire, reverse-reverse-turing-test, minor literature / minor tech-nology, bionic cockroach, deterritorialisation of calculation, becomings, anti-oedipus, minority machine

### non-conclusion — AI colonialism (p. 167–173)

vachnadze explicitly refuses to write a conclusion — *"there is no conclusion, we can't do conclusions anymore"* — and instead offers a reading of muldoon & wu's (2023) paper *artificial intelligence in the colonial matrix of power* as a final frame for everything that came before. the move: everything the book has described — the pastoral apparatus, the computational episteme, the theologico-machinic unconscious — is not just a western problem. it is a *colonial* problem.

**1. the colonial matrix of power (pp. 167-168)**

muldoon & wu identify three axes along which AI operates as a continuation of western colonialism: (1) the *colonial supply chain* — the physical infrastructure of AI from mining to manufacturing to deployment, (2) the *international division of digital labour* — who does the well-paid creative work and who does the invisible, exploited grunt work, and (3) *hegemonic knowledge production* (= the way in which the dominant culture's way of knowing becomes the only way of knowing — western/eurocentric epistemology presented as universal truth, marginalising all other knowledge systems).

these three axes have emerged from western/eurocentric forms of colonial modernity that manifest in highly racialised forms of epistemic-institutional monopolies and unsubstantiated claims to universality. *coloniality* (= distinguished from *colonialism*, which is the historical political domination of one country by another — coloniality is the ONGOING structure of power, culture, and knowledge that persists even after political independence has been achieved. the colony may be "free" but colonial patterns of thinking, valuing, and organising persist) encompasses *self-colonisation* too — particularly prevalent in post-soviet states. the end of political colonisation has not led to the end of economic, cultural, and epistemic colonisation. *"the modernity/coloniality analytic emphasizes that the imaginaries and epistemologies of AI development are inextricably predicated upon rationality and universality and are not divorced from coloniality"* (muldoon & wu, 2023).

**2. the colonial supply chain and ecological devastation (pp. 169-171)**

AI infrastructure requires massive amounts of rare materials — lithium, cobalt — mined under catastrophic ecological and human conditions. the global mining industry places a disproportionate burden on countries like rwanda, ethiopia, south africa, and the democratic republic of the congo. *"a single natural language processing model produced 660,000 pounds of emissions, amounting to as much as five cars over their lifetime"* (muldoon & wu, 2023). the ecological impact is distributed unequally: *"the AI supply chain relies on rare earth minerals from the majority of the world; the process of mining and assembling these minerals follows colonial patterns of uneven global trade in which those closest to the source of the minerals often obtain the least value from their extraction."*

corporate politics occlude (= hide, cover up) the reality of environmental devastation. through eschatological narratives and abstract models, the material impacts of extractivism are hidden from sight. the AI industry with its stories of eco-friendly abundance, algorithmic rebirth, and bionic resurrection is the contemporary mutation of the technocratic, theologico-colonial expansionist project of planetary extractivism.

**3. the international division of digital labour (pp. 171-172)**

the asymmetries go beyond environmental concerns. most highly paid software development jobs are in western countries. the mining industry "thrives" in the developing world with astounding levels of pollution, worker exploitation, and threats of violence against unionisation. natural resources are mined in africa; assembly and manufacturing takes place in asia. these relations of economic dominance are *structural features of the AI industry* — they are not going to change.

most of the unpleasant work — *microwork* (= the fragmented, piece-rate digital labour that involves tasks like removing violent or pornographic content from datasets, labelling images, or verifying data — repetitive, psychologically damaging, and extremely poorly paid), content moderation, physical labour in mining and assembly — is taken up by the invisible, racialised spaces of the global south. india, pakistan, bangladesh, philippines, and indonesia comprise the largest segment of microworkers. *"the work is structured in a way to render invisible the contributions of workers in the majority world from the public imaginary of AI's production, preferencing the so-called highly skilled work of engineers"* (muldoon & wu, 2023). by rendering workers invisible, the AI industry feeds the technocratic delusion that AI is "fully automated" and autonomous.

**4. hegemonic knowledge production and the will to knowledge (pp. 172-173)**

the discourse on AI promotes western and eurocentric views as universal truths while subtly (and sometimes not so subtly) marginalising alternative worldviews and lifestyles. coloniality takes a distinctly epistemic shape here, engaging in a strategy of control that foucault identifies as the *will to knowledge* (= foucault's term for the drive to produce knowledge as an exercise of power — knowledge is never innocent; the desire to know is always entangled with the desire to govern. who gets to define what counts as knowledge, what counts as intelligence, what counts as rational?). formal systems, paradigms, and models have their own political and economic conditions and consequences. no institution is neutral.

*"the knowledge projects that began in europe in the seventeenth century, including the so-called anthropological study of colonised communities, engaged in de-humanising and extractive data collection practices with unidirectional flows of information and data towards europe"* (muldoon & wu, 2023). all "alternate epistemologies" are silenced and ignored. the west only offers recognition to marginalised discourses once apparatuses are already in place to manage them.

the intellectual monopoly of colonial discourse is directly built into the institutional assemblages surrounding AI technology. the racial apparatus has mutated into the "neutrality" of universal truths that privilege multiple forms of structural violence — white supremacy, heteronormativity, male-centred masculinity, gender essentialism, profit-seeking, exploitation — in virtue of the epistemologies that legitimate the status quo. *"the statistical logics underpinning artificial intelligence reveal continuities with racial imperialist views of national progress... the political positions emerging around AI can be traced to the eugenicist conceptualisation of a racialised hierarchy of intelligence that was used to justify european colonial expansion"* (muldoon & wu, 2023).

the discourse that promotes the neutrality and value-freedom of machine learning technologies is merely "re-branded" racism, sexism, resource extractivism, data mining, imperialism, exploitation, and algorithmic governance.

**key concepts**: colonial matrix of power, colonial supply chain, international division of digital labour, hegemonic knowledge production, coloniality vs. colonialism, self-colonisation, microwork, will to knowledge (foucault), ecological extractivism, structural racism in AI
