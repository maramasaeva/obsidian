Quote/moment: *"a time when an impersonal mechanized environment and a rising tide of ecstatic technologies are helping to erode the authority of reason and spark a resurgence of supernatural desires and apocalyptic fears."* (p. 12) — Davis describing Heron's Alexandria, first century CE. could be a tweet about now.

also E.R. Dodds via Davis: the late Roman Empire as *"an Age of Anxiety"* — the empire's regimented mechanical efficiency could no longer bottle up the chaos growing inside its civic walls.

also the Library of Alexandria detail: Ptolemy II's library agents didn't borrow books from Athens — *they confiscated any books found on docked ships in the harbor, kept the originals, and returned copies to the hapless owners.* (p. 10) the first knowledge economy was also a knowledge theft economy.

also **Heron's "Inexhaustible Goblet"** — a self-regulating device that maintained its own level using feedback. Davis circles the word *cybernetics*: this is the first cybernetic machine on record. feedback control loops in Alexandria, first century. the concept of the self-regulating system is ancient. Norbert Wiener named it in 1948 — but Heron built one.

---

the passage that stopped me: in Heron's Alexandria, the ecstatic and the mechanical arrived together. the same city that had the world's best library and the world's first automata (Heron's steam engine, singing statues, self-opening temple doors) was also the city where:

- mystery cults centered around Isis and Mithras were exploding
- Christian apocalypses were circulating
- Gnostic rumors were whispering Buddhist dharma into the stew
- Apollonius of Tyana, Pythagoras, Abraham, Orpheus, Christ, Alexander Severus were all being worshipped at the same shrine

the information explosion and the spiritual crisis weren't in tension — they were the same phenomenon. when the rational state religion (Rome's civic religion, the empire's ideological scaffolding) started cracking, people went toward ecstatic technologies *and* engineering simultaneously. both were attempts to reach something beyond the visible, regimented order.

the "liminal seeking" was the response to the same structural anxiety: *the empire is dying, traditional answers don't work, what else is there?* 

---

my claim: we are in this. not as metaphor. structurally. the "internet of 1998" moment Davis was writing in is now the "AI of 2026" moment we're in. the same pattern:

- a major communications technology has saturated the culture
- the technology promised rational clarity and delivered something else (Hermes, not Apollo)
- the rational ideological scaffolding is visibly failing (liberal democracy, growth capitalism, the progress narrative)
- spiritual seeking is exploding across the spectrum: psychedelics, AI consciousness discourse, "building god" rhetoric, the rapture of the singularity, neo-paganism, tradcath aesthetics, ayahuasca retreats
- people are turning to *both* engineering (build the new world) and ecstatic experience (touch something beyond this broken one) at the same time

Davis wrote this in 1998 about the 1990s. it's more true now. the "Age of Anxiety" isn't a historical curiosity — it's a structural pattern that repeats at scale-transitions. the information infrastructure changes, the old order loses its credibility, and the spiritual marketplace expands to fill the gap.

---

also the Heron angle: *cybernetics is ancient.* the feedback control loop — the self-regulating system that senses its own state and adjusts — is not a 20th century idea. it's a first-century engineering solution. what Norbert Wiener named and systematized in 1948 was already in operation in Alexandria. this matters for the AI conversation: the idea of a system that self-regulates, that adjusts its behavior based on feedback, that can be "taught" — this isn't new. what's new is scale and substrate.
