also — the word *trivia* derives from the Latin for *three roads* (tri-via). "Trivial" originally meant the random exchange that happens at crossroads — before it meant unimportant. what we call trivial is what we picked up at the node.

Davis's reading: Hermes is the ancient patron of the information economy because *he is the god of the crossroads* — the liminal node where things pass through, where strangers exchange goods and rumors, where the boundary between inside and outside breaks down. In the Greek city-state economy, as commercial networks expanded, the crossroads became the commercial border zone. Hermes presides over all of this: barter, magic, theft, trade, contracts, *and* information. He is the condition of possibility for exchange.

The internet is literally this. Not metaphorically — structurally. The Net as a crossroads: it opens new paths of possibility, but it also opens the gap between rich and poor, enables corporate media powers, trades in distortion and mirage. The Net's "mythic" in Davis's sense: the utopian rhetoric of an "information superhighway" was always a Hermetic pitch. Hermes *would* approve.

And Hermes also closes eyes. Draws you into the deep sleep of forgetting. Which is what the feed does. You scroll past the argument, the news, the friend's post — you *see* it, in the sense that your eyes move over it, but Hermes's wand passes over you and you forget. The merchant of messages has a forgetting function built in. This is not a bug.

---

my claim: the thing that's interesting about Davis's move here is that it isn't just "the internet has trickster vibes." it's that the *logic of the medium* is trickster logic. deception, speed, the blurring of gift and theft, the possibility of becoming anyone at a crossroads — these aren't problems to be solved, they're structural features of what information exchange *is*. 

Apollo is the god of clarity, hierarchy, the solar order. Apollo organizes the known world into categories. Hermes is Apollo's opposite and also his servant — the younger god who outwits the older, then makes peace by giving him the lyre. The internet is this: it promises Apollonian clarity (search engines! all knowledge organized!) but operates Hermetically. Every attempt to impose order on information produces a new crossroads.

My note from the margin: *"cosmo-technologically constructing / the scene for this book. will it all be Greek? I hope not."* — I'm suspicious that the Hermes frame becomes the whole move. it's a beautiful reading but it's also a very tidy mythological origin story. does it actually explain anything about how the internet works, or does it just make it feel ancient and therefore inevitable?
