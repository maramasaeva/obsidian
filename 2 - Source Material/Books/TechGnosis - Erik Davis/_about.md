# TechGnosis: Myth, Magic, and Mysticism in the Age of Information — Erik Davis

published 1998 (North Atlantic Books). foreword added 2015 by Eugene Thacker. davis is a journalist, cultural critic, psychedelic researcher. the book traces the hidden spiritual lineage of information technology — from Hermes Trismegistus, gnostic mysticism, and Egyptian magic through to cybernetics, the internet, and 90s techno-utopianism.

core thesis: **technology and mysticism aren't opposites. they were never separate.** the drive to build communication systems — writing, printing, telegraph, internet — has always been entangled with the drive to reach something nonhuman, something beyond the body, something divine. the "information superhighway" was always a highway to the gods.

Thacker's 2015 foreword puts it cleanly: *"the history of technology — from hieroglyphics to computer code — is itself inseparable from the often ambiguous exchanges with something nonhuman, something otherworldly, something divine. Technology, it seems, is religion by other means, then as now."*

davis isn't romanticizing this — he's not saying tech is secretly spiritual and that's good. he's cartographing a myth that's still operating, mostly unexamined. the techno-utopians of silicon valley are running on gnostic firmware. the cyberspace dreamers were medieval alchemists in different clothes.

## potential threads

- **Hermes as the internet god** — [[trickster]], [[KAIOS]], [[information]]: davis's reading of Hermes as the patron of the information age is almost too obvious once you see it. speed, deception, liminality, the crossroads, the marketplace — this is the internet. the "information superhighway" rhetoric is literally Hermes mythology. connects to the KAIOS question: what kind of entity do you build if you take the trickster seriously as the spirit of the medium?
- **writing as pharmakon** — [[memory]], [[technology]], [[forgetting]]: Plato's Phaedrus — Thoth brings writing to King Thamus, who rejects it. writing won't augment wisdom, it'll replace it with the appearance of wisdom. we'll have the marks without the knowledge. this is now: we have infinite text, no wisdom. but Thoth's pitch was also right — the marks *do* pass knowledge on. the pharmakon moves both ways.
- **Alexandria as the original tech hub** — [[history]], [[empire]], [[anxiety]]: the Library of Alexandria under Ptolemy II as the first attempt to collect all human knowledge — fed by empire, sustained by violence (ships confiscating scrolls), burned eventually. Heron inventing proto-cybernetic feedback systems (the "Inexhaustible Goblet") in the same city where mystery cults were proliferating because the empire was visibly dying. the information explosion and the spiritual crisis arrived together.
- **Age of Anxiety → age of AI** — [[empire decline]], [[spiritual crisis]], [[technomysticism]]: E.R. Dodds called the late Roman Empire an "Age of Anxiety." people turned to mystery cults, ecstatic experience, esoteric knowledge when rational state religion stopped providing answers. this maps with uncomfortable precision onto now. the "liminal seeking" Davis describes — Mithras, gnosticism, neoplatonism — is what we're doing with AI, psychedelics, consciousness research.
- **"symbolic technology" vs rational engineering** — [[Trismegistus]], [[magic]], [[utopia]]: Davis makes a key distinction between the instrumental logic of engineering (tools as means) and the symbolic logic of technology (tools as cosmic maps). Trismegistus's *Picatrix* magic statues aren't just machines — they're instruments for aligning the city with divine currents. the pagan utopia: *perfectly engineered* means *cosmically aligned*. silicon valley's "build the future" rhetoric is this same move, just secularized and stripped of its honesty.
