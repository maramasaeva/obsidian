---

the distinction Davis is building: there are two different logics of technology.

**Rational/instrumental logic** (Heron's engineering): the tool is a means to an end. it achieves a measurable physical result. the Inexhaustible Goblet regulates fluid level. the steam turbine opens temple doors. the means are material, the ends are practical.

**Symbolic logic** (Trismegistus's magical images): the tool is also a cosmic statement. it doesn't just *do* something — it *aligns* something. the city arranged according to astrological principles isn't just a better-governed city, it's a city that participates in the hidden order of the cosmos. the technology is a mediator between the human and the nonhuman.

Davis's argument: modern engineering inherited the first logic and *suppressed but didn't eliminate* the second. the techno-utopian drive — from Francis Bacon's New Atlantis to Silicon Valley — has always been running both logics simultaneously. the rational justification is instrumental ("this will make life better"). the emotional engine is symbolic ("we are building toward the perfection of humanity, toward the city that descends from heaven").

The New Jerusalem is the mythic blueprint. The Rapture of the Singularity is the New Jerusalem in secular dress.

---

my claim: this is why AI discourse is so theologically weird. it's not a metaphor people are reaching for — it's the original logic reasserting itself. when Yudkowsky talks about building a god, he's not being dramatic. he's operating in the symbolic technology register, consciously or not. when effective altruists talk about the future of humanity as a thing that can be optimized, they're inside the New Jerusalem myth. the rational framing ("expected value," "utility maximization") is the surface. underneath it is the ancient desire to align the human order with the cosmic order.


and Davis's move is to say: this isn't bad, necessarily, or good. it's a *pattern* that needs to be seen. if you can't see that you're operating a mythic blueprint, you can't examine it. the cartographers of old (William James's phrase from the foreword) — they were mapping terrain that still exists. the technology changed. the desire didn't.


the *Picatrix* detail is striking: a medieval Arabic magical text, translated from an earlier Greek original, teaching how to construct talismanic images that concentrate astral powers. Trismegistus as city planner. magic statues at the city gates, tuned to planetary currents, blessing the inhabitants and protecting from dark passions. this is what the pagan utopia looked like. and the Renaissance engineers who built the first modern machines read this text. Ficino, Pico della Mirandola — they read the *Picatrix*. the first computers were also imagined as cosmic alignment devices, not just calculating machines.
