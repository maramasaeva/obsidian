Quote/moment: *"If you've never wasted an effort, you're filtering on far too high a required probability of success."* (p. ~149)

also: *"you could fail and have everyone think you were stupid to try."* (p. ~149)

also: *"If you only try the things that are allowed for your 'reference class,' you're supposed to be safe — in a certain social sense. You may fail, but you can justify the attempt to others by noting that many others have succeeded on similar tasks."* (p. ~149)

also: *"Several people have now told me that the most important thing I have ever said to them is: 'If you never fail, you're only trying things that are too easy and playing far below your level.'"* (p. 148)

also: yudkowsky names it *anxious underconfidence* — when the fear of public failure is so strong you become a nurse instead of a physicist because you're *sure* you'd succeed at nursing. the problem isn't the nursing. the problem is the certainty. you chose the thing you knew you couldn't fail at, and the cost was never testing whether the other thing was possible.

My claim: yudkowsky says 90% of his in-person advice is telling people to be *less* underconfident. this applies to women, nonbinary people, and low-status people more than to overconfident internet commenters.

the reference class trap: if you only do what "people like you" have done before, you never test outside the class. but the class was defined by the same anxiety. it's circular. the people who tried ambitious things and succeeded are no longer in your reference class — they moved to a different one. so your class keeps getting more conservative.

the mark of the fallacy: not testing. if you refuse to test the prediction that you'd fail — even when testing is cheap, even when failure is survivable — that's the anxiety talking, not the evidence. you're not being modest. you're being afraid.

connects to the think workshop: teenagers slicing off possibilities because the social cost of being seen to try and fail is too high. the workshop could name this — not as motivation but as diagnosis.
