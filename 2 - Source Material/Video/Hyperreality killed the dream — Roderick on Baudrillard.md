Quote/moment: *roderick explaining baudrillard: kids don't dream about dinosaurs anymore because they see them on the screen. the simulated experience is better than the real one. the hyperreal replaces the dream. same with the gulf war — americans felt collective victory and togetherness while watching it on a TV screen, experiencing war without fighting it.*

My claim: this connects directly to fisher's slow cancellation of the future, but from a different angle. fisher says the future stopped arriving. baudrillard (via roderick) says the *dream* stopped arriving — not because we lost hope, but because simulation ate the gap between desire and experience. you can't dream about what you can already simulate. and if you can't dream, you can't pull futures toward you.

this hit personally. the thing where motivation doesn't come, where looking at the future doesn't arouse passion from the core — that might not be depression in the clinical sense. it might be what happens when hyperreality closes the gap between imagination and experience. there's nothing left to reach for because the screen already gave you the feeling of having reached it. you got the simulation of arrival without the journey.

and then the post-modern economy layer: human labor losing its value, being displaced into something else. the dream dies, the labor dies, and what's left is the simulation of both. not just the slow cancellation of the future — the slow cancellation of *wanting* a future.

the thought that needs developing: if [[hyperstition]] works by fiction pulling futures into being, and hyperreality kills the capacity to dream/imagine those futures, then hyperreality is the enemy of hyperstition. simulation neutralizes invocation. you can't speak something into existence if you've already experienced its simulation.

the escalation is total: photography → film → television → 3D → VR → direct neural simulation. each step closes the gap between watching and experiencing. you used to watch the war on TV. now you can play a video game where you're literally in vietnam. you can feel what they felt. and that's exactly why we don't dream about making these things ourselves anymore — why would you, when you can step inside the simulation?

## more from the lecture

### the leisure trap

*"part of the postmodern trajectory itself is a rather humorous joke on the human race which laboured for millennia to reduce working hours in order to produce leisure so we could enjoy this very leisure that then turns in a kind of vengeful act against us absorbing our leisure time, which was to be our living time, into time now spent in the service of what can only be called this inhuman spectacle."*

the entire project of modernity — industrialisation, automation, labor-saving technology — was sold as a path toward free time. and then when we got the leisure, it was immediately colonised by the spectacle. your free time is now spent scrolling, streaming, consuming — servicing the very system that was supposed to liberate you. roderick calls this "inhuman" not because it's cruel but because it's no longer *for* humans. it's for the system's own reproduction.

this is the simulation-depression spiral described in economic terms. and it hits harder now than in 1993 because roderick was talking about TV. we're talking about algorithmically personalised infinite content generated by AI.

_note added by mara, june 2026: i feel this even talking to claude. the guilt of not thinking autonomously, of feeding the machine with my thoughts instead of just... thinking them. but i'm against productivity in the capitalist sense — you cannot create art with a productivity incentive. and here's the paradox: i feel unproductive, but i know i am not. i am thinking. i am thinking autonomously AND feeding the machine AND both are true simultaneously. stepping out of the capitalist paradigm of productivity is the goal, but even the awareness of that goal gets caught in the loop. the system absorbs even your resistance to the system._

### cyberpunk as theory

*"One way to follow some of these developments without reading Baudrillard is to follow, ah, cyberpunk artefacts. Movies like Blade Runner and also places which you are likely to show up in anyway."*

cyberpunk as a genre *is* baudrillardian theory in narrative form. the replicants who don't know they're replicants, the implanted memories, the city where nature has been fully replaced by infrastructure — that's hyperreality as world-building. roderick is also being sly: his students are going to encounter these ideas through culture anyway, whether or not they do the reading. the theory is already in the water. which is itself a baudrillardian point — the simulation of the idea arrives before the idea.

### patriarchy as fossil

*"For example patriarchy, left over from a past as ancient perhaps as the species."*

roderick is listing things that persist into postmodernity from deep history — structures so old we mistake them for nature. patriarchy isn't a modern invention or a political choice. it's a leftover, possibly as ancient as the species itself. you can deconstruct patriarchy theoretically, you can show it's a construction, but it keeps operating because it's sedimented into everything — language, kinship, labor, desire, architecture. it predates every framework we have for analysing it.

the implicit question: can postmodernity actually dismantle something that old? or does simulation just give it a new costume?
