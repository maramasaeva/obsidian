so much of how we think about alignment — and how we think we can solve it — depends on the framing. alignment is defined as a problem. the alignment *problem*. but what if the framing itself is the thing that needs to shift?

western tech culture treats alignment as: how do we make sure AI does what humans want, and doesn't do what humans don't want? and that assumes we know what we want, that we know what's good for us, that human values are the correct reference point. but that's one cosmotechnics. eastern traditions, older traditions, other ways of being in time and space — they wouldn't necessarily frame it as a problem at all. they might say that not aligning AI with human values could actually be a desirable direction. not because human suffering doesn't matter, but because there might be something beyond our current understanding of what suffering and death even are.

i'm not saying this from a place of nihilism or depression. i'm saying it from the recognition that we cannot know everything from inside the human mind. we've decided, from a human perspective, what death is, what pain is, what loss is. but maybe there's something larger behind those experiences that we're just afraid to explore. and i think that fear is what turns alignment into a problem — the assumption that if things go badly for humans, that's the worst possible outcome, full stop.

but people in other cultures already experience time and space differently. already have cosmologies where what we call destruction is part of a larger process. if we were to think from those places, we might not see alignment failure as catastrophe. we might see it as something to explore.

the alignment problem is a problem because we defined it as one. and i want to think about what happens when you don't.
