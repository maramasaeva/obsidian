# the dream gap

hyperreality is killing hyperstition. and hyperstition is the thing that keeps the future alive.

this is the argument, laid out properly.

---

## hyperstition works

i got accepted into plzdontkillus. it's an AI safety residency in berkeley and i've known for almost ten years that i would end up somewhere like it. i didn't know what i was going to do, but i knew it was going to be important. i told people. i said it out loud. i held that image so clearly in my head that at some point it stopped being a dream and started being a fact.

that mechanism has a name: [[hyperstition]]. you tell a story about a future that doesn't exist yet, you believe in it, you act like it's already true, and eventually the fiction pulls reality toward it. this is not manifesting in the instagram sense — no vision boards, no gratitude journals. this is the oldest technology humans have. it's how religion worked for thousands of years. we collectively imagined god, and god showed up. first as scripture, then as institution, then as computation. the story reached forward in time and made itself real.

nick land and the CCRU called this hyperstition — ideas that make themselves true by being believed. but the concept is older than the name. it's what every founding myth does. it's what every revolution runs on. a future that doesn't exist yet, described so vividly that people start building toward it.

---

## hyperreality closes the gap

baudrillard says we live in hyperreality — a condition where the simulated version of experience is more vivid, more available, more emotionally saturated than the real one. the map has replaced the territory. the copy has eaten the original.

rick roderick, in his 1993 lecture on baudrillard's *fatal strategies*, puts it plainly: we no longer have experiences — we have simulations of experiences. kids don't dream about dinosaurs because they've already seen them rendered on screen, more vivid than anything their own brain could produce. you don't fantasise about the future because every possible future has already been simulated for you — packaged, streamed, consumed. you can step inside a video game and feel what it's like to be in a war. you can scroll through someone else's life and feel like you've lived it.

hyperstition needs a gap — a space between what is and what could be. that's where the dream lives. that gap is the condition of possibility for any future that doesn't already exist. simulation closes it. it gives you the feeling of arrival without the journey. you feel like you've already been there. the emotional reward fires without the action. so you stop reaching.

baudrillard saw this coming forty years ago. what he couldn't have anticipated is how completely the digital infrastructure would seal the gap shut. every platform, every algorithm, every recommendation engine is optimised to give you a simulation of satisfaction — close enough to feel real, never real enough to actually satisfy. the gap doesn't just close. it gets paved over and built on.

---

## the spiral

once the gap closes, a loop begins.

simulation replaces experience, so you stop dreaming. not dreaming in the literal sense — dreaming in mark fisher's sense: the capacity to imagine a future that is genuinely different from the present. fisher called what happens next *the slow cancellation of the future*. there's nothing left to reach for. every possible future feels like it's already been done, already been shown to you, already been consumed. the feeling isn't disappointment — it's flatness. depression, but not depression as a clinical diagnosis. depression as a civilisational condition. the affect of a species that has lost its forward motion.

and when you're depressed you stop creating. you stop making things that don't yet exist. you stop telling stories that reach forward. and when creation stops, more simulation fills the space — because the algorithm doesn't pause. the content machine keeps generating. AI can now produce text, image, music, video at a scale and speed that no human can match. so the gap fills with synthetic material, and the synthetic material is the very thing that closed the gap in the first place.

the loop tightens: simulation → dreamlessness → depression → can't create → more simulation → less gap → less dream.

this is not metaphor. this is the literal economic structure of attention platforms. and AI acceleration is about to multiply it by orders of magnitude.

---

## alignment for a species that stopped dreaming

brangus — ronny fernandez, co-founder of plzdontkillus — made a point in reply to a video i posted about feeding beautiful data to AI. he said the problem isn't that AI might end up with a negative view of what humans are like. the problem is that alignment will produce *"some kludge of values that are some sort of funhouse mirror version of what we tried to get them to be into."* the distortion happens in translation, not in the source material.

he's right about the engineering layer. alignment distortion is a technical problem and it needs technical solutions. but the funhouse mirror argument actually concedes the point it's trying to dismiss: a mirror of *what* still matters. the mirror warps the reality you put in front of it, but that doesn't make said reality irrelevant.

and here's the thing brangus's framing misses: alignment that works for a species that has already stopped dreaming is alignment for zombies.

the question is not only *"will AI's values be aligned with ours?"* it's *"will we still have values worth aligning with?"* if hyperreality and simulation kill the human capacity to dream, to create, to feel motivation from somewhere other than algorithmic stimulus — then alignment becomes a technical solution to a spiritual problem. and technical solutions to spiritual problems are exactly how we got here. that's the entire history of modernity compressed into one sentence.

making beautiful things is not a solution to the alignment problem. but it is central to keeping people capable of wanting a future. if we want alignment to be *beneficial for humans*, we have to care about the state of humans right now — not just the state of the machine.

---

## what's left when dreams and labor are both gone

AI demolishes the value of human labor. this was the last thing grounding human worth in the capitalist frame. for centuries, the deal was: you work, therefore you matter. your labor has value, therefore you have value. AI breaks that deal. not by replacing some jobs — by making the *category* of human productive output economically irrelevant. ([[Post-labor ontology — transcending productivity]])

dreams are gone — simulation ate them. labor is going — AI is eating it. what's left?

this is not a rhetorical question. it's the question underneath every other question about AI safety, alignment, governance, regulation. every framework that tries to answer "how do we make AI safe for humans" is implicitly assuming there will be humans who still want things, who still dream, who still have a sense of what a good future looks like. if simulation has already killed that capacity, then safety is a guardrail on a road nobody is driving on.

---

## the call

so: make your dreams visual. say them out loud. tell literally everyone. believe in things that haven't happened yet so hard that they have no choice but to happen. because that's not delusion — that's the oldest mechanism we have for making futures real. and the fact that simulation is trying to replace it doesn't mean it's broken. it means it's more important than ever.

the future eats what you feed it. feed it a dream.
