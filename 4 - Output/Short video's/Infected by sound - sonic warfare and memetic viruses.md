## Script

So there's this book called Sonic Warfare by Steve Goodman — you might know him as Kode9, he runs Hyperdub. And there's this chapter about the viral properties of sound. About how memes — not internet memes, the original concept from Dawkins — are basically non-genetic pattern replicators. they copy themselves from brain to brain the same way a virus copies itself from cell to cell. and the book makes this point that the transmission isn't just digital. it's physical. a virus can pass through printing ink, through light rays in the human lens, through nerve impulses, through finger muscle contractions. it doesn't need a computer. it just needs a path.

and there's this concept in the book from L. Ron Hubbard actually, via Burroughs, called the engram. and the engram is basically an injurious moment that's literally recorded by the body. not by the brain — by the body. at the cellular level. a permanent trace left by a stimulus on the protoplasm of the tissue. and the book says these engrams contain everything — they're like a phonograph record, they capture sight, sound, smell, taste, organic sensation, all of it. and if they stay in place, if they're not processed, they predispose you to psychosomatic illness, mental disorders, and always something less than complete psychophysiological sanity.

and I keep thinking about that in the context of what's happening now. because we talk about social media and doomscrolling as if it's just a bad habit. like it's about time wasted or attention lost. but what if it's actually more like infection? what if the content we consume is literally writing itself into our bodies? not just our brains — our nervous systems, our stress responses, our sleep patterns, the way our fingers move without us deciding to move them?

because think about it — when you scroll through rage-bait, through doom, through content designed to spike your cortisol, that's not just information passing through you. it leaves a trace. it changes your baseline. you're a little more anxious after. a little more numb. a little less able to sit still. and that trace accumulates. day after day after day. it's not a metaphor to call that viral — the mechanism is the same. stimulus, trace, replication. the content reproduces itself through your behavior. you saw it, it changed you, and now you produce more of the same kind of signal.

and the book makes this point about memetic transmission being transversal — it cuts across genetic lines, across generations, across borders. it's more viral than genetic. and that's exactly what's happening on platforms. an idea, a mood, an aesthetic, a piece of rage — it doesn't travel down a family tree. it travels sideways. through feeds. through algorithms. through a person with a platform who has a hundred thousand followers and whose one bad take infects all of them in an afternoon.

but here's the thing — and this is the part I keep coming back to — not all viruses are destructive. that's not how virology works. some viruses are neutral. some are even beneficial. your gut biome is full of them. and if harmful ideas can spread virally, if doom can infect a population through sound and image and vibration — then so can the opposite. a positive idea, a piece of real art, a moment of genuine connection — that can spread the same way. through the same channels. through the same finger muscle contractions.

so the question isn't how do we stop the virus. you can't. the infrastructure is the infrastructure. the question is: what virus are you carrying? what are you replicating? when someone encounters you — your posts, your art, your voice, your presence — what trace do you leave on their body? what engram are you writing into them?

because you're always transmitting. you're always a vector. the only choice is what you're a vector *for*.
