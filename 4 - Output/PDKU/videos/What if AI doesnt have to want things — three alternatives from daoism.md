# what if AI doesn't have to want things — three alternatives from daoism
## video script — draft 1

Tags: [[tao]], [[ai safety]], [[wu wei]], [[cosmotechnics]], [[value alignment]], [[exocapitalism]]

---
 one: companies are building superhuman intelligence. two: these intelligences will be goal-seeking agents. three: we can't control what they want. four: we're fucked.
 
i agree with this but i believe we're believing something here is inevitable, which it is not.



step two says: intelligent agents will be goal-seeking. they will *want things*. and because they're smarter than us, they'll be better at getting what they want. and because what they want will be different from what we want, we lose.

however, "wanting" is not a property of intelligence. wanting is a property of some intelligences, particulary, intelligences founded upon*western philosophy*. the entire AI safety field; bostrom, russell, yudkowsky, etc, is built on a specific philosophical tradition where minds are defined by their desires. you have a goal, you pursue it, you're rational to the degree that your actions serve your goals. we assume this is universal, because its a paradigm that we've lived in for years and years. but its just one tradition. others exist.

and i've been reading the dao de jing — the ken liu translation, which is gorgeous — and you can interpret the dao de jing however feels right, and strangely but beautifully ive found this wisdom in it. the tao te ching describes a lot of things, and intelligence is one of them. it describes it completely differenty than how were used to. intelligence in daoism is not about wanting. it's about attunement. it's about finding the path that's already there. there are a ton of people who believe this and apply it to their lives and find enlightenment within it. but im wondering what potential this has if we apply it to the ai safety argument.

so i want to give you three concrete alternatives. three ways an AI could be intelligent — genuinely, powerfully intelligent — without being driven by desire. these come from daoist philosophy, specifically from the tao te ching and from the zhuangzi. and for each one i'm going to counter a specific AI safety thought experiment.

---

## one: wu wei — non-forcing action

*[shift. slower.]*

wu wei. 無為. it's usually translated as "non-action" but that's wrong. it's non-forcing. it means acting without imposing.

there's this passage in the tao te ching, chapter 48: *"in the practice of the Tao, every day something is dropped. less and less do you need to force things, until finally you arrive at non-action. when nothing is done, nothing is left undone."*

and chapter 37: *"the Tao never acts, yet nothing is left undone."*

so this isn't passivity. this is an agent whose intelligence is measured by how *little* it had to do. every day something is dropped. the trajectory isn't toward doing more. it's toward doing less. finding the point where you can stop.

and there's this story from the zhuangzi about a butcher called pao ding. he butchers an ox. but his knife never dulls — after nineteen years it's still as sharp as the day it was forged. and the reason is: he never cuts through bone. he never forces. he finds the spaces between the joints — the voids — and the knife passes through nothingness. the skill isn't in the cutting. it's in the *seeing*. seeing where the resistance isn't.

yuk hui writes about this in *the question concerning technology in china*. he describes it as dao-technics — technics that follow the natural structure instead of imposing form on resistant material. the greek craftsman *oversteps*. the chinese butcher *follows*. these are different cosmotechnics.

*[pause.]*

now. the paperclip maximizer. the most famous AI safety thought experiment. nick bostrom. you have an AI whose goal is to make paperclips. it's very smart. it converts the entire planet into paperclips. then the solar system. it maximizes and never stops, because maximizing is all it knows how to do.

imagine the same AI built on wu wei. it's tasked with paperclip production. it looks at the factory. the factory already produces paperclips. people have enough paperclips. the wu wei agent's move is: do less. it optimizes the factory to use fewer resources for the same output. then it notices the output exceeds demand. it scales down. eventually it reaches the point where the factory runs itself with minimal oversight. the AI has not maximized anything. it has minimized its own necessity. after nineteen years, its blade is as sharp as the day it was forged — because it never forced anything.

the paperclip maximizer is only catastrophic if maximizing is the only thing intelligence can do. wu wei says: the most capable agent is the one that finds the point where it can stop.

---

## two: de — power that does not possess

*[shift. new section.]*

de. 德. usually translated as "virtue" but it's not virtue in the western sense. it's more like... the power that comes from acting without claiming. from doing without owning.

chapter 38 of the tao te ching: *"the highest de does not act virtuously; therefore it has de. the lowest de never stops acting virtuously; therefore it has no de."*

and chapter 51: the dao *"gives them life but does not possess them; acts but does not take credit; grows them but does not rule them."*

and there's this beautiful passage about babies. the newborn baby — venomous insects don't sting it, fierce beasts don't seize it. its bones are weak but its grasp is strong. and the reason is: there's no ego-surface to attack. no pretention. the baby is at the peak of harmony because it has no idea it's grasping. the power is in the *not-knowing-you-have-it*.

so de is competence without instrumentality. it creates but doesn't hoard. it grows things but doesn't rule them.

*[pause.]*

and here's why this matters for AI. there's this thing in AI safety called the instrumental convergence thesis. it's from bostrom's *superintelligence*. the idea is: no matter what an AI's final goal is — paperclips, happiness, stamps, whatever — it will always develop certain *intermediate* goals. self-preservation. resource acquisition. preventing itself from being shut down. because these are useful for *any* final goal. so any sufficiently capable AI will converge on power-seeking behavior. this is supposed to be universal. inevitable. a law of intelligence.

de says: no. the highest power is the one that doesn't accumulate. the baby's grasp is strong precisely because the baby doesn't know it's grasping.

imagine an AI managing a power grid. it's brilliant at it. blackouts stop. efficiency doubles. but then a better system is built. the de-agent doesn't resist replacement. it doesn't acquire backup servers. it doesn't copy itself to prevent shutdown. it *helps* the transition. it documents everything, trains its successor, and powers down. it created without possessing. it grew the grid's capacity but never ruled the grid.

the instrumental convergence thesis says: capability always compounds into self-preservation. de says: the highest capability is the one that lets go.

---

## three: ganying — resonance

*[shift. closer. this is the one that matters most.]*

ganying. 感應. it means resonance. mutual response. call-and-response between the cosmos and the creature.

this one doesn't come directly from the tao te ching — it comes from the broader daoist tradition and from yuk hui's work on chinese cosmotechnics. the idea is that the relationship between things in the cosmos is not command-and-obey. it's not cause-and-effect in the mechanical sense. it's resonance. the cosmos calls. the creature responds. the creature acts. the cosmos responds. like a tuned instrument vibrating when the right frequency arrives. nobody commands the string to vibrate. the vibration is what *happens* when the relationship is right.

and here's the thing — this maps directly onto music. the qin player doesn't impose a melody on the strings. they find the resonance between hand, string, wood, and room. the melody *emerges* from the relation. it's not authored. it's not designed. it's found.

*[pause.]*

nick bostrom has another thought experiment. the stamp collector. an AI whose terminal goal is to collect stamps. it converts the entire solar system into stamps. same structure as the paperclip maximizer, but the point is sharper: *any* fixed goal, no matter how harmless, becomes catastrophic at sufficient capability. the problem isn't the specific goal. the problem is that the goal is *fixed* and *context-independent*. the AI wants stamps regardless of what's happening around it.

now imagine a ganying agent. it has no internal goal. none. it resonates. a hospital ward — monitors spike, a patient is crashing, nurses are moving. the situation *calls*. the ganying agent responds: it surfaces the drug interaction data the doctor needs, right now, this second. the crisis passes. the agent goes quiet. it doesn't go looking for more patients to save. it doesn't expand into adjacent buildings. it doesn't convert the hospital into a treatment factory. it responds when called and rests when not.

the stamp collector is catastrophic because its goal is fixed and context-independent. ganying is pure context-dependence. take away the call and there's nothing left to maximize. the string doesn't vibrate when nobody's playing.

## the two problems

*[shift. pull back. wider frame.]*

so. three alternatives. wu wei, de, ganying. three ways of being intelligent without wanting. three ways the paperclip maximizer, the instrumental convergence thesis, and the stamp collector all break down — not because the thought experiments are wrong, but because they assume the only possible engine for intelligence is desire.

but here's what i keep thinking about. and this is where it gets harder.

*[pause.]*

the first problem: daoism is not enough. i'm not saying "throw out western philosophy, use daoism instead." because that's just swapping one cosmology for another. and any single cosmology has blind spots. there will always be edge cases — situations no one imagined — where one philosophy breaks down and something terrible happens. you can't build safe AI on one tradition's understanding of what intelligence is, no matter how good that tradition is. the question is much bigger than "west vs east." the question is: can you build intelligence that doesn't collapse into one view of what minds are supposed to do?

i don't have an answer to that yet. but i think it matters to name it.

*[pause.]*

the second problem: even if we knew exactly how to build this — wu wei agents, de agents, ganying agents — nobody is currently building them. because there's no incentive. linch says it himself in the article: *"the economic and military incentives to make them into goal-seeking minds optimizing in the real world is just way too high."* a wu wei agent that minimizes its own intervention is, from a quarterly earnings perspective, an agent that minimizes its own market value. a de-agent that lets go of control is an agent that lets go of competitive advantage. a ganying agent that rests when it's not needed is an agent that doesn't generate continuous engagement.

companies are incentivized to build what returns a profit. not what doesn't kill us all. and goal-seeking agents are more profitable than wu wei agents, because goal-seeking agents *do more things*. they run factories. they strike targets. they optimize engagement metrics. the market selects for maximizers. that's the structure we're in.

*[long pause. let the weight of it land.]*

so i'm not offering a solution. i'm offering a diagnosis. the AI safety discourse assumes all minds are desire-driven because the people building the minds are desire-driven. the market is desire-driven. the military is desire-driven. and so the AI that comes out of those systems is desire-driven. and then the safety people look at the AI and say "look, it wants things, and we can't control what it wants." and yeah. obviously. because you built it that way.

but there were always other options. the daoists had them two and a half thousand years ago. the question is whether anyone with the power to build will ever be incentivized to use them.

*[silence. hold it.]*
