# Symbolic Technology vs Rational Engineering
## Video Essay Notes — Angles, Perspectives, Approaches

---

## The Core Tension

"Symbolic Technology vs Rational Engineering" is essentially: AI is built with rational engineering methods — optimization, measurable loss functions, reproducible systems — but it operates entirely in the domain of **symbolic meaning**, which is fuzzy, contested, cultural, alive. That gap is not incidental. It **is** the alignment problem. The video has a natural spine in this.

---

## Angles & Perspectives

### 1. The Symbol Problem as the Alignment Problem
The hardest version of the case: AI alignment is difficult to talk about because the problem lives in the space between symbol manipulation and meaning-making — and we don't have good language for that gap. Open with the Chinese Room (Searle), updated: it's not that the system doesn't understand, it's that *understanding* is the wrong category for what it's doing. Alignment tries to encode human values into a system that only ever does symbols, never meaning. The difficulty isn't technical — it's ontological.

### 2. Why It's Hard to Talk About (Meta-angle)
The difficulty of discussing AI alignment is structural: every time you try to describe it, you use language — which is itself a symbolic system — to describe a problem about symbolic systems. It's self-referential in a way that makes it slippery. The conversation keeps eating its own tail. Strong audiovisual angle: you can literally demonstrate this in how the video is made — the explanation becoming what it's explaining.

### 3. Whose Rationality (Queer / Feminist Epistemology)
"Rational engineering" is culturally coded. The optimization targets, the benchmarks, the notion of what counts as a "good" outcome — these come from specific people in specific places with specific assumptions. The alignment problem reveals that the "objective" system was never neutral. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) — whose human? The "human" in "human values" is contested before you even start. This is the political angle and it's underexplored in mainstream alignment discourse.

### 4. The Glitch as Epistemic Signal
Hallucinations, refusals, jailbreaks, weird outputs — these aren't bugs. They're **the symbolic tension becoming visible**. Where rational engineering fails, something bleeds through. Connects naturally to glitch aesthetics — the moments of breakdown are structurally important, not incidental. Build the whole video around AI failure cases as the actual data, the actual thing to look at.

### 5. Music Theory as Analogy
Equal temperament is rational engineering imposed on music — the octave divided into 12 equal semitones so instruments could play in any key. Practical engineering solution. But it sacrificed just intonation — the natural harmonic series, the "true" intervals. The irregular frequencies in Ableton are the symbolic dimension returning. The analogy for AI: you can rationalize the system perfectly and still lose something irreducible about meaning. Alignment is the just intonation problem for intelligence.

### 6. Composition vs Engineering as Epistemics
You don't "solve" music. You develop taste, tradition, style, community, argument over time. No one expects to write a function that outputs a good album. What if AI alignment requires composition epistemics rather than engineering epistemics? Speculative but generative — opens into art and practice honestly rather than decoratively.

### 7. Map-Territory All the Way Down
Rational engineering assumes you can make the model (map) correspond to reality (territory). But human values aren't territory — they're maps interpreting maps. There's no ground truth to align to. Alignment assumes a fixed target; the target is moving, contested, political. The hard problem isn't building the system — it's that the destination was never fixed.

### 8. The Unreasonable Ineffectiveness of Mathematics for Meaning
Inversion of Wigner's observation: mathematics is unreasonably *effective* at describing the physical universe. But unreasonably *ineffective* at describing what things mean, why they matter, what we should do about them. Rational engineering works beautifully in the domain where math is effective. The alignment problem is the collision with the domain where it isn't.

## Structural Options for the Video

**Option A — Inside-out**: Start with why it's hard to talk about. Make the meta-difficulty the entry point, work outward to the substance. The video opens in the difficulty, not in explanation.

**Option B — Through the glitch**: Use AI failure cases as the through-line. Each glitch is a lesson. You're not explaining the problem — you're watching it happen. More essayistic, more visual.

**Option C — The music/alignment parallel**: Lead with the sound design — let the irregular frequencies be the first argument. The body knows before the mind understands. Then build the conceptual parallel from there.

---

## For the Audiovisual Dimension

The irregular frequencies are already making an argument — they're the symbolic interrupting the rational in real time, in the listener's nervous system. That's not decorative. Make it explicit in the script: *the sound is the thesis*. Glitch visuals, AI artifacts, distortion can do the same work visually — not as aesthetic but as epistemology. The thing you're saying and the way you're saying it are the same thing.

---

## Keeping Viewers Gripped — Personal Involvement

The goal isn't to explain the problem from outside it. It's to make the viewer realize they're already inside it.

### Strategies

**Implicate them from the first line.** Don't start with AI. Start with something they've already done — accepted an autocomplete suggestion, felt something was off about a chatbot's response, wondered why an algorithm keeps showing them something. They're already participating in this system. The video is about something they're already doing.

**Use the second person strategically, not constantly.** One well-placed "you" lands harder than ten. "Every time you accept a suggestion without reading it, you're part of the feedback loop" — say that once, let it sit.

**Create productive discomfort, not resolution.** The instinct in a video essay is to explain and resolve. Resist it. Leave some things open and uncomfortable. Alignment is unsolved — the video should feel that. The viewer should finish it slightly more uncertain than when they started, but in a way that feels generative, not nihilistic.

**Make the stakes present-tense.** This isn't about future superintelligence. The alignment problem is already shaping hiring decisions, content moderation, medical diagnosis, who gets a loan, what you see first. Ground it in now.

**The political angle makes it personal for people erased by "neutral" systems.** Queer people, people of color, people with accents, people whose names break autocorrect — they already know the system wasn't built for them. The video can acknowledge this without being didactic. Just name it and move on. The acknowledgment is the thing.

**The somatic hook.** The irregular frequencies bypass the question of whether the viewer is intellectually engaged. The body responds before the mind decides. This is a real rhetorical tool. Use it consciously: certain moments in the script should land on a frequency that stings, not for shock, but because the argument is visceral and the sound should be too.

**The self-referential move.** At some point the video itself should become aware of what it is — a symbolic technology trying to describe the limits of symbolic technology. This can be subtle, a single line, a visual glitch at the right moment. But it closes the loop in a way that makes the viewer feel the whole thing was one coherent object, not just information.

**The open ending.** Don't conclude. Pose the last thing as a question, not an answer. The viewer should walk away holding something unresolved that they now feel responsible for.

the thing that keeps me stuck on this — and i've been stuck on it for a while — is that i don't know how to talk about it without the problem getting inside the explanation.

like, every time i try to describe what's wrong with AI alignment, i'm using language. i'm using symbols. to describe a problem about symbols. and the conversation just starts eating itself.

*[AV: frequency shift — something slightly off from what came before, just enough to register in the chest.]*

in the 1700s, musicians had a problem. keyboard instruments — harpsichords, early pianos — couldn't play in every key. each key had slightly different tuning, slightly different feel. some intervals were beautiful and some were, genuinely, unbearable. the mathematics of sound doesn't divide cleanly into twelve equal pieces. it just doesn't.

so they engineered a solution. equal temperament. they took the octave and divided it into twelve exactly equal semitones. every key now works. you can modulate anywhere. it's elegant, it's rational, it's a genuinely impressive piece of design.

and it costs something you can't measure.

what it costs is called just intonation — the natural harmonic series, the intervals that arise from the physics of vibrating strings. the "true" fifth. the interval that physically resonates because the frequencies are in a simple whole-number ratio. equal temperament's fifth is close. it's 2 cents flat from pure. that's almost nothing. 

and your nervous system knows.

*[AV: frequency drops to something sub-bass or near-inaudible, something felt more than heard.]*

that's what i'm working with in the audio on this. the frequencies that are just slightly wrong. irregular. unresolved. i want something to sting without you knowing why it's stinging.

because that's where i think we actually are with AI. not in the clean version of the problem. in the two-cents-flat version. close enough that it functions. different enough that something in you knows.

*[AV: cut. cleaner visual. something that reads as "rational" — data, grids, clean lines.]*

AI alignment is the project of making AI systems do what we want. beneficial to humans, safe, honest. that's a reasonable summary of a field that hundreds of very serious people have dedicated their lives to.

and the problem — the reason it's so hard to talk about clearly — is that it sounds solvable when you say it like that. it sounds like an engineering problem. specify the values, constrain the behavior, verify the output. this is what engineers do.

the thing is — values aren't a specification problem.

john searle had this thought experiment in 1980 called the chinese room. you're in a room with a book of rules. someone passes you chinese characters through a slot. you look up the rules, pass back the right characters. to the person outside, you speak chinese. inside the room, you understand nothing. you're manipulating symbols according to rules. which is exactly what every AI system does, at every layer, all the way down.

searle got a lot of pushback on this. i'm not even sure the chinese room is the right frame. but the question it surfaces is real: what's the difference between processing symbols correctly and understanding what they mean?

and the alignment problem is: what if there isn't one, for these systems? what if they can pass every test, produce every right answer, and still be doing something categorically different from what we think we're asking?

---

here's what doesn't get said enough.

when we talk about aligning AI to "human values" — whose values?

the training process for these systems, for the big ones you're using right now, involves something called reinforcement learning from human feedback. people rate outputs. thumbs up, thumbs down. the model learns what humans prefer.

which humans.

the people doing the rating are predominantly from specific countries, specific income brackets, specific cultural contexts. the people designing the preference criteria — even more specific. a small group of people at a small number of companies in a small number of cities making decisions about what "good" looks like for systems that billions of people use.

donna haraway called this the view from nowhere — the pretense of objectivity that's always actually a view from somewhere very specific, just refusing to say where.

the system is not neutral. it was never neutral. "rational engineering" is culturally encoded. the benchmarks reflect choices. the choices reflect people. the people had interests.

and if you've ever had autocorrect refuse your name. if you've ever had a content filter catch your queer slang as a slur. if a hiring algorithm has scored your resume lower for reasons you'll never see — you already know this. you didn't need me to say it. you felt the two cents flat.

*[AV: frequency sting — one of the sharp ones.]*

---

the thing about glitches is that we treat them as noise. as the system failing to do the thing.

but what if the glitch is where the thing becomes visible?

when an AI hallucinates — confidently states something false as fact — it's not a malfunction exactly. it's the system doing exactly what it was trained to do, pattern-completing, and hitting the limit of what symbol manipulation can do without meaning behind it. the hallucination is the rational process running clean. it's the absence of something that shows up as error.

when a jailbreak works — when someone's prompting makes a model do what it wasn't supposed to — it's revealing a seam. the values weren't built in. they were laid on top of something that doesn't have values, and the laying on top was imperfect, and the imperfection has a shape.

rosa menkman talks about glitch as a site of information. the error tells you something about the system that the system can't tell you about itself. 

every hallucination is data. every refusal is data. every time the system produces something uncanny, something almost right but wrong in a way that bothers you — that's the symbol problem surfacing. that's the gap between manipulation and meaning showing up in the output.

*[AV: slow build in the audio here — frequencies layering, not chaotic but accumulative.]*

---

wittgenstein — late wittgenstein, the useful one — said that meaning is use. that words don't carry meaning the way bags carry groceries. meaning is what happens when language is embedded in a form of life. in practice. in shared activity. in the whole context of being the kind of creature that does things and cares about things.

AI systems don't have forms of life. they were trained on the traces of ours. they learned the shapes of what we say without ever being inside the situation of saying it.

that's not a solvable engineering problem. it's an ontological one. you can't specify your way into having a form of life. you can't add enough RLHF to bootstrap participation in human meaning.

so what do you do?

i don't know. i'm not going to pretend i have the answer to the thing that hundreds of researchers are actively working on without resolution.

but i think the first thing is to stop talking about it like an engineering problem. because the frame shapes what solutions look like. if it's engineering, you specify, test, iterate. if it's something else — if it's more like the composition problem, the music problem, the problem of developing taste and tradition and community over time — then the thing you need isn't better specifications.

it's different epistemics. a different way of knowing what you're even trying to do.

the reason this is hard to talk about — the real reason, not the polite one — is that talking about it clearly requires admitting that the most powerful technology we've ever built is operating in a domain we don't understand, in service of values we haven't agreed on, for purposes that are still being decided by people with interests they're not required to disclose.
