# you can't specify your way into a form of life
## video script — draft 2

Tags: [[alignment]], [[symbolic technology]], [[emergence]], [[art]], [[forms of life]], [[simulation]], [[experience]]

---

so i've noticed that when non-AI people — or just like regular people — talk about AI they kind of seem to miss something about how AI specifically is made. which is understandable. but it's not made the way that other software is made.

when you write software or programs you write rules. mostly if-then statements. you specify behavior and the software does what you tell it. but AI isn't written like that. you just give it huge amounts of data, you give it time with that data, and then something emerges from that. so there are no instructions — well, there are, but it's more about exposure. so AI knowledge is emergence. these systems, they're grown. we don't build them.

and i'm just wondering — if that's true, if you can grow a system that has real emergent knowledge of language and context and reasoning, then why is alignment not seen as something that you can grow? this is something i've been coming back to lately.

---

that's also why i think solving alignment has nothing to do with engineering. and engineering, well, that's kind of how most engineers are obviously tackling the problem. but this isn't an engineering problem. you don't have to specify values and constrain behavior and verify if the output is correct. it's more of an ontological problem.

so then what do you do, obviously? i don't have a full answer. no one has a full answer. but i think if the frame is wrong you just need different epistemics.

---

so i do have this hypothesis that i've been thinking about quite a lot recently. and it's a hypothesis, it's not a solution. i proposed it to nate soares, who we had a little chat with in the attic at PDKU a couple days ago, and he didn't fully agree with me — which is fine. i still felt very honoured to even be able to propose the idea to him.

but i do think this idea is worth saying out loud. because i've noticed that other fellows here do quite agree with me. and as an artist, and as someone that has to represent artists in this discussion, i feel like what i'm saying here is valuable. so please, if you're critical about this, try to view it from an artist's point of view. artists are really very powerful people in history. it's worth taking them seriously in this.

---

you don't teach a child what is good or bad through a book of rules. obviously some parents try — just tell them: do this and not that. but that doesn't work in pedagogy. it is known that knowledge about things like ethics and morality comes through experience. the child falls on the ground and someone helps them back up. they take something that's not theirs and they see the other person start crying. they receive love and they feel what that's like from the inside. the opposite as well — they feel hurt and they feel what that's like from the inside.

so these values, they emerge — from being in situations, from feeling what happens, from connecting with others, with other people, other species, nature, the planet itself. and that's also why your values are shaped by things that happened before you were even born. because we carry the memories and the scars of the people before us. their experiences also inform what we now find ethical and valuable.

so if humans develop moral knowledge through emergence — which is not controversial — then why is emergence considered, well, i wouldn't say irrelevant, but it's not viewed as like the essential central topic of interest when we talk about alignment? if you can grow knowledge through exposure to data, then why can't you grow values through exposure to experience?

---

and so what i keep thinking is: what if instead of giving an AI data about human experience — which is what has been done up until now — instead you put the AI inside human experience. not metaphorically. actually. and maybe this might be problematic, but — simulating the full breadth of human situations and putting the AI inside each of them. not as an observer but as an active participant.

and with these experiences i mean all of it. joy, loss, belonging, even violence, hunger, loneliness, transcendence, spirituality. situations from the past as well as the present, because the present only is not the whole of human value — these are values we've learned over time. and i mean situations from every age, every culture, every context. including contexts that western modernity completely disagrees with. that has to be included. you can't build something that's supposed to understand human value by only sampling the value systems of people in a specific place at a specific time.

and i think — and maybe that's naive, i'm open to criticism — a system that had experienced that full breadth of human life would not just make one decision about what is good or bad. it would have a sense of: well, depends. depends on the situation, what's at stake, who is there and what this person needs. which is actually a more sophisticated moral understanding than most current systems have.

---

obviously we can't directly simulate human experience in a way that a machine can inhabit. i don't even know what it would mean for a machine to actually be inside a situation in the relevant sense. and i think that in and of itself would be a very unaligned thing for humans to do — it would feel wrong. it would not be very respectful towards the AIs we'd put in that situation.

so this is why i've tried to find something that represents all of human experience in a more embodied way. more embodied than data. and i think art is the answer.

art has existed since the beginning of recorded human time. and art is kind of a proxy for experience. a novel puts you inside a life you've never lived. a painting can hold a feeling that can't be contained by anything else. and music — especially music — it does something to you before you've even decided to let it happen. it does something to your nervous system. and film makes you afraid for people who don't exist.

art is literally how humans have always tried to transmit the interior of experience across the gap between one person and another. and crucially, art is cross-cultural, cross-temporal. every human situation that has ever existed has left traces in art, in music, in storytelling, in ritual, in the things humans made when they needed to say something that couldn't be said directly. and i think that's such a powerful tool that we're completely underestimating.

so what i'm thinking is not just art — but everything that a real artist, in heart and soul and blood, would classify as being important to artistry. so not just the product of art, not just the music, the painting, the book — it's more the interactions as well. conversations, stories, everything that humans have imagined and expressed, everything that leads to pieces of art being created. not as data to be processed. as experience to be inhabited.

---

and this i think would mean that the people most relevant to working on alignment are not engineers, and not philosophers. they're artists. they're historians, anthropologists, people who understand what human experience actually contains and how to get it across that void of misunderstanding.

when i brought this up with nate he raised the real objection, which is: even if you simulate these experiences, does the AI actually experience them in any meaningful sense? or is it just processing data shaped like experience? and is emergence even possible for moral knowledge the way it is for linguistic knowledge?

those are serious objections. i'm not dismissing them. but i still think the hypothesis is worth taking seriously. because the alternative — RLHF, specification, guardrails — is not working at the level the problem actually lives at. and it seems strange that emergence is the mechanism by which these systems develop everything else, but alignment is the one thing that has to be hard-coded from outside.

---

i'm just very much thinking out loud. and i'm trying to get enough people to think about this with me. i really hope this means something to anyone.

i've never felt any moral responsibility for a big issue before. but this one — it feels like it lands for a big part on my shoulders. because there are not many people in my position, with my personal experiences, with behavior that was considered not ethical even when it was just caused by humans. i think it's important to have people like me working on this.

that's why i'm using my voice.
